This file grounds the group-monitor skill (a changes-only briefing on the
papers pipeline, repositories, students, and services) and the meeting-scribe
skill (meeting notes into org). It covers how the group leader meets, records,
monitors, and sets expectations; it does not cover the science itself.

## What the evidence says

### One-to-one and group meetings

- Flexible working hours do not remove the PI's duty to supervise: "We must hold periodic meetings with lab members to check the progress of their work." Skipping them is a known source of frustration for graduate students [maestre2019].
- Progress meetings also tell members that the PI is paying attention, and they are the place to discuss solutions when expectations are not met [maestre2019].
- Meetings and discussions that involve all lab members, retreats, and time for side projects foster within-lab collaboration [maestre2019].
- Decisions taken face to face look arbitrary to people who were not present; leaders should record and communicate the results of in-person activities in a form everyone can read [sholler2019].
- Discussing lab practices in group meetings is one way to build a common culture, and project goals should be re-evaluated on an ongoing basis rather than fixed once [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Grove treats meetings as the medium of managerial work and the one-on-one as the subordinate's meeting: the subordinate sets the agenda, the manager mostly listens and learns, both keep notes, and open items carry over in a hold file (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Grove ties the frequency of one-on-ones to task-relevant maturity: newer people or newer tasks need more frequent contact, experienced people on familiar work need less (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Barker advises a regular individual meeting with every member plus a group meeting on a fixed schedule, and treats consistency of schedule as more important than the format chosen (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [barker-at-the-helm].
- The HHMI/BWF guide devotes chapters to lab meetings, one-on-one mentoring, and project management, and recommends scheduled meetings with agendas and written follow-up (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves].

### Written expectations and transparency

- Onboarding should cover general lab procedures, research goals, and each person's responsibilities and expectations; the long-term success of a lab's practices depends on continued training and re-evaluation [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Governance should be explicit so that people know who decides what, and the rules should be agreed before a contentious issue arises, not during one [sholler2019].
- A code of conduct is only useful with a trusted reporting path and enforcement; reports should go to a party independent of the people involved, and anyone implicated should recuse themselves [sholler2019].
- Guidelines on what counts as a contribution and how it is acknowledged should be adopted and published, not left implicit [sholler2019].
- The PI should discuss coauthorship openly and train members to evaluate merit; dictating authorship, or insisting on authorship of every paper, breeds distrust [maestre2019].
- Barker puts expectations in writing at the start: hours, notebooks and data, authorship, presenting at lab meetings, and how and when the member hears from the PI (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [barker-at-the-helm].
- The HHMI/BWF guide recommends written expectations and mentoring agreements between PI and trainee (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves].

### A healthy-lab culture

- Well-being is a PI priority: be kind, ban harassment and discrimination, be sensitive to personal and health situations, and listen [maestre2019].
- Let people set their own schedules and judge them by outcomes, not by hours present [maestre2019].
- Show gratitude, and give rapid feedback on requests and drafts; the paper gives no turnaround number but calls speed a priority [maestre2019].
- Treat members as teammates with informed opinions, and delegate real work; top-down labs limit critical thinking [maestre2019].
- Do not compare lab members with one another or with your younger self; the PI's job is to help each person reach their own goals [maestre2019].
- Respect working hours, holidays, and vacations; long hours should be the exception, and the PI should model this [maestre2019].
- Normalize rejection and celebrate successes; discuss the reasons for a rejection and how to avoid it next time [maestre2019].
- Support professional development: training, networks, conference travel, and letting students and postdocs cosupervise [maestre2019].
- Newcomers should be welcomed proactively, with a named point of contact and starter tasks reserved for them [sholler2019].

### Good research practice as the group standard

- Good research practice covers planning (question, protocol, sample size, data plan, bias), execution (avoiding questionable practices, interpreting significance), and reporting (report everything, follow guidelines) [schwab2022].
- A written and registered protocol reduces publication and hindsight bias; deviations remain possible but must be documented and reported [schwab2022].
- Preregistration does not fit purely exploratory work, which should be reported as exploratory rather than forced into a confirmatory frame [schwab2022].
- A data management plan written before data collection describes what is collected, how it is stored and protected, and where it goes afterwards [schwab2022].
- Questionable practices such as low power, repeated peeking, selective reporting, and post hoc hypothesizing are often used without bad intent; people need to know them to avoid them [schwab2022].
- Report all findings, including negative ones; a nonsignificant result is not evidence of no effect [schwab2022].
- The rules "can (and should) be implemented as a habit in everyday research, just like toothbrushing" [schwab2022].
- Culture change is needed because incentives reward quantity over protocols, data sharing, and negative results [schwab2022].

### Information management in the lab

- A common culture comes first: shared vision of objectives, shared language, and mutual expectations on timelines [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Focus on current projects first; do not spend limited effort cataloguing the past except on demand [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Create the record before the artifact exists, give records a status such as to do, in progress, completed, or canceled, and append data as work proceeds [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Use stable computer-generated identifiers as the link between a thing and its record; human-readable names are secondary [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Manage data before collection starts; link data to the protocol version that produced it; notebook entries should record context, interpretation, and the next step [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Standardize workflows, document processing pipelines and software versions, and use version control [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Avoid data silos, keep data in three locations (two on site, one in the cloud), and never delete primary data; data is lost when computers are replaced and people leave [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Maintaining an information system "is not a one-time event"; all members must contribute, new members need extensive training, and the lab must keep discussing what works [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Keep information on one topic in one place and define the purpose of each communication medium; remove or clearly mark outdated material [sholler2019].
- Organizing study artifacts in a structured way lets data and code be reused within the lab and beyond [schwab2022].
- Locally, the group keeps one org file per student or staff member with dated meeting headings, a staff.org roster with contract end dates and graduation estimates, and a papers.org pipeline whose TODO sequence is READY_TO_SUBMIT, SUBMITTED, REVISING, PAUSED, TODO, then PUBLISHED and CANCELED as done states [org-conventions-local].

### Onboarding newcomers

- Help newcomers judge fit: state the skills needed and guide them to tasks they can handle [sholler2019].
- Legitimate peripheral participation: newcomers join through simple, low-risk tasks that still further the group's goals [sholler2019].
- Getting set up is the first contribution experience; automate and document setup, because friction here is a major barrier [sholler2019].
- Follow up on both success and failure: help the newcomer find the next task, and ask leavers why; keep those conversations confidential but publish the conclusions and corrective actions [sholler2019].
- Evidence on formal mentoring programs is mixed; one study found mentored developers active earlier, another found program entrants less likely to stay [sholler2019].
- Onboarding should be thorough and repeated for every new member, or the lab drifts back to bad practice [pcbi-2023-lab-information].
- Barker treats hiring as a defined process (role defined before advertising, interview for fit, references, lab involvement) and the first weeks as when expectations are set (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [barker-at-the-helm].
- The HHMI/BWF guide has a staffing chapter covering job descriptions, interviewing, and orientation of new hires (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves].

### Managerial leverage, indicators, and monitoring

- Grove defines a manager's output as the output of the organization under them; managerial leverage is the effect of one managerial act on many people's output, and a short one-on-one is high leverage (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Grove recommends a small set of indicators reviewed regularly, and pairing indicators so that over-reaction to one is checked by another (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Grove's monitoring principle is inspection at the lowest-value stage and by sampling: look at plans and early drafts rather than finished work, and vary inspection intensity with the person's track record; delegation without monitoring is abdication (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Grove's management by objectives fixes a few objectives per period with observable key results (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [grove-high-output-management].
- Barker stresses noticing problems early and having the difficult conversation while it is still cheap, with specifics about behavior and a written record of what was agreed (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [barker-at-the-helm].

## Rules we adopt

1. Hold a one-to-one with every student and staff member at a fixed cadence agreed with that person in writing; the member sets the agenda, the PI listens; after each meeting the meeting-scribe writes a dated heading into that person's org file with what was discussed, what was agreed, and what is due next (from [maestre2019], [grove-high-output-management], [barker-at-the-helm], [org-conventions-local]).
2. Hold a group meeting on a fixed schedule and record every decision in writing where non-attendees can read it (from [maestre2019], [sholler2019], [barker-at-the-helm]).
3. Keep one roster of record, staff.org, with a contract end date and a graduation estimate for every member; the group monitor reads people data from this file only and flags approaching dates (from [org-conventions-local], [sholler2019], [pcbi-2023-lab-information]).
4. Every paper in papers.org has exactly one state from the fixed TODO sequence and a last-touched date; the group monitor reports state changes and papers untouched for longer than a threshold the PI sets (from [org-conventions-local], [pcbi-2023-lab-information], [grove-high-output-management]).
5. Monitor artifacts, not people: the briefing reports changes in papers, repositories, and services, and never ranks members against one another (from [grove-high-output-management], [maestre2019]).
6. Report changes only, and inspect in depth where it is cheapest: plans, outlines, and early drafts rather than finished manuscripts; inspect more often for new members and new projects (from [grove-high-output-management]).
7. Concerns about an individual go to the PI alone and into that person's file; they never appear in group channels, in the group meeting, or in messages to peers (from [sholler2019], [maestre2019]).
8. Write the group's expectations once, in one place, and give them to every newcomer: working-hours norms, authorship criteria, data and code conventions, meeting duties, and how to raise a concern (from [barker-at-the-helm], [sholler2019], [maestre2019], [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves]).
9. Give every newcomer a written onboarding checklist with a named point of contact besides the PI, a reserved first task, and a documented environment setup; the checklist is complete when every item is ticked (from [sholler2019], [pcbi-2023-lab-information]).
10. Every project starts with a written plan (question, data, method, evaluation) before results exist; confirmatory evaluations are fixed in the plan and exploratory work is labeled as such in the paper (from [schwab2022]).
11. Every project has a data management plan at the start, uses version control, stores data in nonproprietary formats in three locations, and never deletes primary data (from [schwab2022], [pcbi-2023-lab-information]).
12. Report all findings including negative ones, and state every deviation from the plan in the manuscript (from [schwab2022]).
13. The briefing lists every item waiting on the PI (drafts, requests, reviews) so that feedback stays fast; the PI sets the turnaround target because the literature gives none (from [maestre2019]).
14. When a member leaves, run a hand-over checklist for data, code, documentation, and unfinished manuscripts, tied to the contract end date in the roster; the exit conversation stays confidential but its lessons go into the group's written practices (from [sholler2019], [pcbi-2023-lab-information], [barker-at-the-helm]).
15. Review the group's working rules and information practices in a group meeting periodically and after every onboarding round; the review is recorded like any other decision (from [pcbi-2023-lab-information], [schwab2022]).

## Where sources disagree

- Autonomy versus monitoring: [maestre2019] says let people set their own schedules and judge outcomes; [grove-high-output-management] says a manager who delegates without monitoring has abdicated. Maestre himself adds that flexibility does not remove supervision. We follow both: full autonomy over hours and place of work, combined with fixed-cadence one-to-ones and a written trace, and monitoring of artifacts rather than presence.
- Indicators versus not comparing people: [grove-high-output-management] wants regular indicators; [maestre2019] warns against comparing lab members. We resolve this by indexing indicators on papers, repositories, and services, never on per-person output, and by reporting changes rather than league tables.
- Mentoring: [maestre2019], [barker-at-the-helm], and [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves] promote active mentoring; [sholler2019] reports mixed evidence on formal mentoring programs. We keep the mechanisms that all sources share (a named contact, follow-up after the first contribution, exit conversations) and do not treat a mentoring program as sufficient by itself.
- Keep versus remove: [pcbi-2023-lab-information] says never delete data; [sholler2019] says remove or mark outdated documentation. These concern different objects. We never delete primary data or meeting records, and we mark stale guidance as outdated rather than deleting it.
- Preregistration: [schwab2022] recommends registered protocols but concedes they do not fit exploratory work, which is common in computational biology. We require a written plan for every project and registration only where a confirmatory claim is made.

## Not covered

- No source gives a number for the cadence of one-to-ones or group meetings, or for feedback turnaround. Grove ties frequency to experience but gives no rule that transfers to doctoral supervision. Cadences and thresholds are the PI's choice, recorded per person.
- No source addresses monitoring of doctoral progress against time to degree, contract ends, or graduation estimates; the roster fields come from local convention only.
- No source treats git repositories, continuous integration, or running services as management indicators; that mapping is ours.
- No source describes the structure of a meeting record beyond "write it down"; the meeting-scribe format is a local decision.
- Remote and hybrid supervision is touched on only as home working in [maestre2019]; there is no guidance on distributed groups.
- Institutional procedure at KAUST for conduct, misconduct, or contract matters is outside all sources; the skills point to the procedure and never replace it.
- The papers show no evidence on the effect of automated briefings on a group; the group-monitor skill should be judged by whether the PI acts on it, and that has to be observed locally.
