## What the evidence says

### Where the seven dimensions come from

- The rubric in `brain/rubrics/progress-assessment.md` has seven rows:
  research question and plan, evidence velocity, methodological rigour,
  writing progress, milestone standing, communication, and independence. Each
  row is traceable to a source below; the row set itself is a group choice.
- Domain A of the Vitae framework, knowledge and intellectual abilities,
  covers the knowledge base (subject knowledge, research methods in theory and
  in practice, information seeking and management), cognitive abilities
  (analysing, synthesising, critical thinking, evaluating, problem solving)
  and creativity (inquiring mind, insight, innovation, argument construction,
  intellectual risk). Those descriptors sit behind the first three rows
  [vitae-rdf].
- Vitae domain C, research governance and organisation, covers professional
  conduct, research management including project planning and delivery, and
  risk management, which is where the rigour and milestone rows draw their
  vocabulary [vitae-rdf].
- Vitae domain D covers communication and dissemination and working with
  others, behind the communication and writing rows [vitae-rdf].
- Milestone standing is not a judgement at all but a rule check: the
  programme, the track and the start date fix the deadlines, semesters are
  counted by a stated rule, and a missed deadline under the current rules
  means dismissal rather than probation [kaust-cemse-milestones].
- Committee composition, petition timing and document lead times are equally
  fixed by the programme rules, so an artefact either exists by the required
  date or it does not [kaust-cemse-milestones, kaust-registrar-program-guide].
- The National Academies frame any such review as support for a conversation
  in a relationship with a power difference, not as a verdict on a person
  [nap2019-mentorship].

### What a level means for the rigour row

- Good research practice starts from a narrow and clear research question,
  identified against what is already known, before anything is run
  [schwab2022].
- A written protocol fixed in advance is the practice that separates a planned
  analysis from one shaped by its results; registration is standard in
  clinical work and has been proposed more widely [schwab2022].
- Sample size deserves an explicit justification, because an underpowered
  study is likely to miss an effect that is really there [schwab2022].
- A data management plan describes the life cycle of the data, including
  storage, and is written rather than assumed [schwab2022].
- Bias is a systematic deviation of an estimate from what it estimates, and
  the more of it there is the less valid the conclusion; the risk of bias is
  something to be assessed rather than asserted [schwab2022].
- Questionable research practices, such as adding samples until a test becomes
  significant, are often used without bad intent, which is why naming them is
  part of training rather than of misconduct policy [schwab2022].
- Dichotomising results into significant and not significant is seldom
  appropriate, invites cherry-picking, and exact values with effect sizes
  should be reported instead [schwab2022].
- Reporting all findings, including the ones that did not work, is part of the
  practice; selective reporting is the file drawer effect [schwab2022].
- Sutherland and colleagues' tips for interpreting claims cover the same
  ground from the reader's side: variation and chance, no measurement being
  exact, the prevalence of bias, sample size, controls and randomisation,
  regression to the mean, base rates, the difference between correlation and
  causation, the risk of extrapolation, and the difference between no effect
  and a non-significant result. The article was not fetched and this entry is
  from general knowledge [sutherland2013].

### What a level means for the practice rows

- Good enough practice in computing names concrete, checkable habits: save the
  raw data, back it up in more than one place, record every step used to
  process it, use a unique identifier per record, and deposit data in a
  repository that issues a DOI [wilson2017].
- For software: an explanatory comment at the top of every program,
  decomposition into functions, no duplication, meaningful names, explicit
  dependencies and requirements, an example or test data set, and code
  deposited where it can be cited [wilson2017].
- For project organisation: one directory per project, named after it, with
  documents, raw data and metadata, generated results, source code and
  compiled programs in separate directories and file names that say what a
  file is [wilson2017].
- For change tracking: back up everything a human created as soon as it is
  created, keep changes small, share them frequently, and use a version
  control system [wilson2017].
- For collaboration: an overview of the project, a shared to-do list, an
  agreed communication route, an explicit licence, and a citation file
  [wilson2017].
- The notebook rules add the record side: every entry dated with a subject, a
  record of how every result was produced in enough detail to reproduce it,
  version control for models and code, a table of contents, and the notebook
  treated as the institution's record rather than personal property
  [schnell2015].
- Schnell also argues that a computational scientist's notebook covers
  thinking and meetings, not only runs, because ideas that are not written
  down are lost [schnell2015].
- Reproducibility practice puts a provenance record behind every result and
  prefers a script over a manual step, so that a figure can be regenerated
  [sandve2013].

### Judging reasoning rather than vocabulary

- The revised taxonomy makes an objective a verb plus a noun: the verb names a
  cognitive process (remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create)
  and the noun names a kind of knowledge (factual, conceptual, procedural,
  metacognitive) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- The level a student is working at is shown by the verb they can honestly
  perform on their own work: recalling the method is a different level from
  explaining why it is the right method here, predicting what would break it,
  or judging whether the result supports the claim
  [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Metacognitive answers are the independence signal: a student who can say
  what they do not yet know and what they will do about it is planning their
  own work [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- A meeting that only asks what was done tests remembering and applying and
  never reaches evaluating or creating, so it cannot show whether the student
  understands their own project [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- The taxonomy is for diagnosing tasks, not for labelling people: a cell
  describes a task [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Gu and Bourne place ownership of the project and its direction with the
  student early, which is the behaviour the independence row is trying to
  observe [gu2007].
- Marino and colleagues treat a written plan with dates, reviewed with the
  supervisor, as central to finishing, which is what the question and plan row
  looks for [marino2014].

### Indicators and their counter-indicators

- Grove's rule for any output measure is to pair each indicator with a
  counter-indicator, so that speed is not bought with rework, and to monitor
  at the stage where the least value has been added
  [grove-high-output-management].
- The same notes warn that delegation without monitoring is abdication and
  that the amount of structure a person needs depends on their experience with
  the specific task, not their seniority [grove-high-output-management].

## Rules we adopt

1. Score the seven rubric dimensions and no others, on a 1 to 5 scale, using
   the descriptors in `brain/rubrics/progress-assessment.md`. Adding a
   dimension is a change to the rubric file, not a decision inside one report.
   (from [vitae-rdf], [nap2019-mentorship])
2. Every score cites at least one evidence id. A dimension with no evidence is
   recorded as `no evidence`, never as a low score. Enforced by
   `rubric_report.py`, which exits 2 when a score lacks an id or cites an
   unknown one. (from [nap2019-mentorship], [grove-high-output-management])
3. Score milestone standing from computed dates only: the programme, track and
   start date determine the deadlines, and the report states the semester
   counting rule and that the registrar's calendar holds the exact date.
   Never score a milestone from memory. (from [kaust-cemse-milestones],
   [kaust-registrar-program-guide])
4. Score rigour against named practices and not against results: is the
   question written, is there an analysis plan fixed in advance, are baselines
   present, are seeds, data versions and a run manifest recorded, are negative
   results reported. (from [schwab2022], [wilson2017], [sandve2013])
5. Score the record, not the tool: a dated entry that says how a result was
   produced, in enough detail to reproduce it, counts as evidence; an
   undocumented result does not, whatever it shows. (from [schnell2015],
   [sandve2013])
6. Judge reasoning by the verb the student can perform on their own work, and
   record the question asked, the level reached and the date. Write the level
   as a property of the answer, never as a label on the student.
   (from [anderson-krathwohl2001])
7. Vary the cognitive level of the questions prepared for the review: at least
   one that asks the student to explain a choice, one that asks what would
   break the result, and one metacognitive question about what they do not yet
   know. (from [anderson-krathwohl2001], [gu2007])
8. Pair every count with its counter-indicator in the report: commits with
   reopened work or reverted changes, words written with sections that
   survived review, meetings held with action items closed.
   (from [grove-high-output-management])
9. State every threshold in the report as a group choice with its number: 21
   days without an artefact for the velocity row, 90 days to a milestone
   without an artefact for a flag, 28 days as the default digest window, one
   semester for an evaluation. None of them comes from a source.
   (from [grove-high-output-management], [kaust-cemse-milestones])
10. Write the report for Robert only, mark it `privacy:local-only`, and never
    show a row, a score or the rubric itself to a student. A review supports a
    conversation and does not replace one. (from [nap2019-mentorship])
11. Never rank students against each other and never place two students in one
    report; a comparison invites a judgement the evidence cannot support.
    (from [nap2019-mentorship])
12. Use Vitae domain labels as vocabulary for the dimensions and never
    reproduce the framework text, its descriptors as a whole, or its graphics.
    (from [vitae-rdf])

## Where sources disagree

- Practice versus outcome: Schwab and colleagues judge research by whether the
  planned practices were followed [schwab2022], while the programme rules
  judge by whether an artefact exists on a date
  [kaust-cemse-milestones]. We score both and keep them in separate rows,
  because a student can be doing careful work and still be behind, and the
  remedies differ.
- How much rigour is enough: Wilson and colleagues explicitly aim at good
  enough practice reachable by everyone [wilson2017], while Schwab and
  colleagues describe a fuller standard including protocols and sample-size
  justification [schwab2022]. We set level 3 at good enough practice and
  reserve level 5 for the fuller standard.
- Whether a taxonomy should be used to judge a person at all: the revised
  taxonomy is explicitly for diagnosing tasks and not for grading people
  [anderson-krathwohl2001], while a progress rubric does produce a score per
  person. We resolve this by scoring the work and the artefacts, recording the
  question and the answer as evidence, and keeping the score away from the
  student.
- Measurement itself: Grove wants indicators and holds managers to them
  [grove-high-output-management], while the mentorship report warns that
  measurement in a power-asymmetric relationship distorts what it measures
  [nap2019-mentorship]. We keep the indicators, keep them Robert-facing, and
  require a counter-indicator for each.

## Not covered

- Whether the 21-day and 90-day thresholds, the 1 to 5 scale, and the seven
  dimensions are calibrated against anything. They are group choices, and no
  source here supports them. They exist to make a review repeatable, not to be
  accurate.
- How to weight the dimensions against each other, or how to combine them into
  an overall judgement. We do not combine them.
- Field norms for velocity in ontology, machine learning and biomedical work,
  which differ enough that one threshold cannot fit all projects in the group.
- How to score a student in a phase with no visible artefacts by design (deep
  reading, a long training run, a stalled collaboration).
- Any inference about ability, motivation or personal circumstances. The
  rubric scores artefacts and recorded conversations only.
- Sutherland and colleagues' article was not fetched; the summary here is from
  general knowledge and should be checked before it is quoted.
