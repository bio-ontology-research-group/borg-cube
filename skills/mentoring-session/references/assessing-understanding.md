A 1:1 that only asks what was done tests recall and execution. This reference
gives the question forms that show whether a student understands their own
project, and the vocabulary for recording what a question revealed.

## What the evidence says

- An objective is a verb plus a noun: the verb names a cognitive process (remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create) and the noun names the kind of knowledge (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive). The pair, not the vocabulary a student uses, says what level the reasoning reached (from the book notes) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Retention needs remembering; transfer to a new problem needs the other five processes. A student who can recall a method is at a different level from one who can explain why it fits here, predict what would break it, or judge whether the result supports the claim (from the book notes) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Metacognitive knowledge includes knowing what one does not know. A student who can say what they do not yet understand and what they will do about it is planning their own work (from the book notes) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Alignment is the working tool: activities and assessment should sit in the same cell as the objective, and the commonest fault is teaching a procedure and then assessing recall (from the book notes) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- A cell of the table describes a task, not a person; the taxonomy is for diagnosing what someone can do, not for grading who they are (from the book notes) [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Asking a student about a method, about the interpretation of results, and about possible next steps is how a supervisor gauges the student's level of understanding; presenting a relevant paper is another way to see it [crossley2025].
- Open-ended questions such as "What do you think might be going wrong?" and "What would you try next?" put the reasoning back with the student instead of handing them the answer [crossley2025].
- Ask directly what the student is stuck on and whether anything should be explained differently, so that admitting confusion is a normal move rather than a confession [crossley2025].
- Dialogic talk that asks the student to justify a choice or connect a result to the argument is the part of supervision that moves the work; telling and status checking crowd it out unless the questions are prepared (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- A student stuck at a conceptual threshold cannot be moved by pressure for output. Recognising the threshold, naming it and scaffolding the crossing is the supervisory response (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- A thesis is an argument, and weak theses fail on data theory and contribution, meaning why the evidence is adequate and what is new. Questions aimed at those two are the ones that predict trouble early (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].
- Originality has many legitimate forms (a new method, a new dataset, a known method on a new problem, a synthesis, a replication in a new setting); a student who believes only one counts will stall (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].

## Rules we adopt

1. Prepare four to six questions before every 1:1, each tagged with the process and knowledge type it targets, and cover at least three different processes. A meeting whose questions all sit in remember and apply is not an assessment of understanding (from [anderson-krathwohl2001], [wisker-good-supervisor]).
2. Every question is tied to a piece of the student's own work with a locator, so the answer can be checked against the artefact (from [crossley2025], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd]).
3. Include one metacognitive question in every meeting, for example what the student is least sure of and what they will do about it (from [anderson-krathwohl2001], [crossley2025]).
4. Include one evaluate or create question whenever there is a result on the table, for example which result they would defend least, or what experiment would falsify the claim (from [anderson-krathwohl2001], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd]).
5. Ask before telling. Give the answer only after the student has attempted it, or when the missing piece is a fact they had no way to know (from [crossley2025], [wisker-good-supervisor]).
6. Record what a question revealed as a dated observation about the work ("could not yet say why the baseline is the right comparison, 3 September"), never as a label on the student (from [anderson-krathwohl2001]).
7. When the same gap appears twice, treat it as a conceptual threshold: name it in the meeting and set a smaller scaffolded step rather than repeating the request for output (from [wisker-good-supervisor]).
8. Do not stack questions in one cell across meetings either; check the last two entries before writing the next set (from [anderson-krathwohl2001]).

## Question forms by process

- Understand, conceptual: explain why this method answers the question; what does this result mean in your own words; how does it compare with the baseline.
- Apply, procedural: what would you change if the dataset were half the size; walk me through what happens when this input is malformed.
- Analyze, conceptual: which part of the pipeline is the result most sensitive to; what would have to be true for this conclusion to be wrong.
- Evaluate, metacognitive: which of your results would you defend least, and why; what would a reviewer attack first.
- Create, conceptual: design the experiment that would falsify your claim; what is the next question if this one is answered.
- Metacognitive: what are you stuck on; what do you not yet understand well enough to write down; what should I explain differently.

## Not covered

- How to score the level reliably. The taxonomy is a planning and diagnostic tool, and the sources give no rubric for judging an answer in conversation.
- Questions for a student who is not yet at the stage of having results, beyond reading and reproducing published work.
- Language effects: how to distinguish limited understanding from limited fluency in English when the student is answering in a second language.
