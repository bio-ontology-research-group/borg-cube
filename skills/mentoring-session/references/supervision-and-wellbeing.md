This file grounds the individual supervision meeting: how often it happens,
what belongs in it, how the supervisor talks in it, and what a supervisor may
and may not do when a student is struggling.

## What the evidence says

### The meeting itself is the supervision

- Regular meetings are recommended across the supervision literature; weekly or fortnightly check-ins for full-time work "provide structure, help catch problems early", and turning up week after week shows the student that the work matters [crossley2025].
- Meetings should not be skipped because a student seems independent, and early-stage meetings should be more frequent to support onboarding and build confidence [crossley2025].
- A meeting reviews progress, troubleshoots the current problem, and clarifies the next steps before the student leaves; one-to-one meetings have the highest reported impact on students and are prioritised over group formats [crossley2025].
- Flexibility about hours and location does not remove the supervisor's duty to hold periodic meetings that check progress; not holding them is itself a source of frustration for graduate students [maestre2019].
- Regular scheduled supervisions from the first weeks, with a written record of what was agreed and what is due by when, are what turn supervision into a managed process rather than a series of conversations (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Students expect the supervisor to have read the submitted work before the meeting, to be constructively critical, and to run the meeting so that ideas are exchanged rather than instructions issued (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].

### How the supervisor should talk

- Supervisory dialogue splits into telling, checking in on routine and welfare, and dialogic talk that makes the student think; most meetings drift toward the first two, and it is the open question asking the student to justify a choice or connect a result to the argument that moves the work (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Students hit conceptual thresholds and appear stuck at them; the supervisor's job is to recognise the threshold, name it, and scaffold the crossing rather than demand output (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Instead of solving the problem, ask open-ended questions such as "What do you think might be going wrong?" or "What would you try next?", and resist micromanaging each step [crossley2025].
- Scaffolding is heavier at the start, with targets and assignments between meetings, and lighter toward the end so the student practises independent decisions and mistakes inside a supportive frame [crossley2025].
- Asking a student questions about a method, the interpretation of results and possible next steps is how a supervisor gauges the student's actual level of understanding [crossley2025].
- Much of what supervisors expect is tacit (what a literature review is for, how much reading is enough, what a chapter draft looks like), and students cannot meet expectations they cannot see; the recurring instruction is to make the tacit explicit (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Supervision styles range from directive to hands-off, students differ in what they need, and an unnamed mismatch produces frustration on both sides; the style should be discussed openly and adjusted as independence grows (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Supervision is described in the literature through a small set of concepts, which include functional project management, enculturation into the discipline, critical thinking, emancipation of the student, and the developing quality of the relationship; a supervisor tends to work mostly within one and to have blind spots in the others (from knowledge of the framework, not verified against the text) [lee2008-supervision].
- The doctorate has predictable stages, including the isolation and confusion that follow the end of coursework and the long middle in which momentum drops; a supervisor who knows the stages can name the stage for the student and act before it becomes a stall (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].

### Expectations, and the mismatch that breaks supervision

- A considerable share of PhD students report that the programme did not meet their original expectations, which is why expectations should be aligned before the student joins [jabre2021].
- The paper's advice is explicit about what to align: funding and its contingencies, professional development opportunities, how many publications are expected and when, meeting frequency, turnaround time on drafts, and working style [jabre2021].
- Most supervision failures are explained by the mismatch between two lists of expectations, the supervisor's list for the student and the student's list for the supervisor (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].
- A negotiated learning contract at the start (roles, meeting frequency, how drafts are exchanged, turnaround times, what the supervisor will and will not do, how disagreements are handled) is revisited at every stage transition (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Questions a prospective student is advised to ask current students are the same questions a supervisor can ask about their own practice: how often do you meet, what is the turnaround on a draft, how does the supervisor react to mistakes or unexpected results, how do they react when personal life interrupts research [jabre2021].

### Wellbeing is part of the supervisor's job, diagnosis is not

- Graduate students have disproportionately higher rates of depression and anxiety than the general population, which is the headline finding cited across this literature [evans2018, jabre2021].
- A large survey of PhD students reported widespread dissatisfaction alongside enthusiasm, including workload, work-life balance and mental health concerns and gaps between expectation and experience (headline finding only; the article was not fetched) [woolston2019].
- Surveys report that a noticeable minority of non-principal-investigators describe their labs as competitive, stressful or toxic, and that mental health problems in academia are rising [maestre2019].
- The wellbeing of group members is presented as a first-order duty: be sensitive with personal, family and health situations, listen carefully on any matter affecting the work, and make it clear that you are there to help remove obstacles [maestre2019].
- Working beyond normal hours, on weekends and during holidays should be the exception, not the rule; the associated stress is named as a main reason for rising mental health problems among early-career researchers [maestre2019].
- Do not compare group members to one another or to yourself as a student; comparison raises stress and anxiety and lowers performance, and the mentor's role is to help each person reach as far as they can and want [maestre2019].
- Rejection of papers, grants and applications is continuous at every career stage; normalising it (sharing your own rejections, discussing why this one happened) reduces impostor feelings, and successes should be celebrated when they occur [maestre2019].
- Setbacks, confusion and feeling like an impostor should be normalised explicitly, with the supervisor admitting what they do not know, and with recurring questions such as "What is something you are stuck on?" [crossley2025].
- Small wins matter for a first-time researcher (a finished figure, a concept understood, a bug found), and acknowledging them in the meeting costs nothing and sustains motivation [crossley2025].
- Rapid feedback on requests, questions and drafts is itself a form of respect that group members notice and value [maestre2019].
- Noticing changes in engagement and referring the student to institutional support is part of the supervisor's job, while diagnosis is left to professionals (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Predictable problem cases (the student who disappears, the student who cannot write, the failed project, a personal crisis, a broken relationship with the supervisor) should be acted on early and with the department, rather than absorbed privately (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Students' lives interrupt the research, and part-time, international and distance students need adjusted contact patterns rather than the standard one (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Isolation and loss of momentum are the predictable dangers, and structures (reading groups, writing workshops, student seminars) are the remedy rather than optional extras (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].

### Credit, authorship and power

- Insisting on authorship regardless of contribution, or dictating author order, benefits only those in power and breeds distrust; authorship should be discussed openly with the people who did the work [maestre2019].
- Group members should be treated as teammates with informed opinions about the work they do, not as executors of instructions; top-down hierarchies limit critical thinking [maestre2019].
- Skill development, not the deliverable, is the primary responsibility of a supervisor, and a project that yields no publishable result but a more capable student is a success [crossley2025].
- Professional development is part of supervision: time and resources for training, building an own network, and supervising more junior students [maestre2019].

## Rules we adopt

1. Every student has a recurring 1:1 in the calendar, weekly or fortnightly for full-time work, and it is not cancelled because the student looks independent. A cancelled 1:1 is rescheduled, not skipped. Robert enforces; the group monitor flags a student with no dated meeting entry in the configured window. (from [crossley2025], [maestre2019], [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate])
2. The agenda is built from evidence that exists before the meeting (commits, drafts, notebooks, the previous entry's open action items), and every agenda point names its evidence. No agenda point rests on an impression. Checked by the mentoring-session skill. (from [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd])
3. Read the student's submitted work before the meeting. If it was not read, say so at the start and move the discussion of it to a named date. (from [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd], [maestre2019])
4. At least half the prepared questions are open questions that ask the student to justify, explain, predict or judge their own work; status questions are allowed but do not fill the meeting. Checked by the mentoring-session skill. (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [crossley2025])
5. When a student appears stuck, name the likely conceptual threshold or process stage before asking for more output, and scaffold the crossing with a smaller next step. (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd])
6. Make the tacit explicit in writing: what counts as a good draft, how much reading is enough, the turnaround time Robert promises on a draft, and what the student is expected to bring to a 1:1. These live in the expectations document, not in a supervisor's head. (from [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], [jabre2021], [wisker-good-supervisor])
7. Every meeting ends with the next steps stated back by the student, and each action item is recorded with one owner and a date. The record is written the same day into the person's org file. Checked by the mentoring-session skill. (from [crossley2025], [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate])
8. Every meeting acknowledges at least one thing that went well when there is one, by name and specifically. Praise is not deferred to the end of a project. (from [crossley2025], [maestre2019])
9. Never compare one student with another, in a meeting or in a written record. Compare a student only with their own plan and their own previous work. (from [maestre2019])
10. Wellbeing signals are reported to Robert as a flag with the observed evidence (a missed meeting, a changed working pattern, a stated overload) and never as a diagnosis, a label, or a claim about a person's health. The supervisor's action is to ask, to reduce load, and to point at institutional support. (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [maestre2019], [evans2018])
11. Overwork is not treated as commitment. Weekend and holiday work is the exception; a pattern of it becomes a topic in the 1:1, not a compliment. (from [maestre2019])
12. Rejections and failed experiments are discussed as normal events with a next step, and the supervisor names their own recent rejection when it helps. (from [maestre2019], [crossley2025])
13. Health, family, visa, contract, salary and grade matters never enter the meeting record, a bead, or any briefing. They stay between Robert and the student, and the record says only that a personal matter was discussed if the student agreed to that much. Checked by the mentoring-session skill. (from [maestre2019], [evans2018])
14. Authorship and credit are discussed openly in the 1:1 as soon as a paper has a shape, and the agreed position is recorded in the meeting entry. (from [maestre2019])
15. The expectations document and the meeting rhythm are revisited at every stage transition (start, qualifying exam, proposal, first paper, writing up) and whenever the relationship shows strain. (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [jabre2021])
16. Every 1:1 record notes at least one skill the student practised or needs to practise, so that skill development stays visible next to output. (from [crossley2025], [maestre2019])

## Where sources disagree

- Meeting frequency: the short-project rules recommend weekly or fortnightly meetings for full-time work and more at the start [crossley2025]; the lab-health rules stress autonomy over schedules while keeping periodic progress meetings [maestre2019]; the supervision handbooks want a fixed rhythm from the first week (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate, wisker-good-supervisor]. We keep a fixed weekly or fortnightly slot with the student free to bring the agenda, because a fixed slot costs little and its absence is the failure mode the sources agree on.
- Who owns the agenda: the handbooks describe supervisions the supervisor plans and records (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], while the wellbeing literature treats the meeting as the student's space to raise what they are stuck on [maestre2019, crossley2025]. We build the evidence-based agenda in advance and open the meeting with the student's own items, which take priority when time runs short.
- Directive versus hands-off: the short-project rules and the handbooks both warn against over-direction but also against absence [crossley2025, wisker-good-supervisor]. We name the current style in the expectations document and shift it deliberately across the degree rather than drifting.
- Long hours: the lab-health rules discourage overwork but respect a researcher's choice to work long hours as their own autonomy [maestre2019]; the survey findings tie workload to poor mental health [evans2018, woolston2019]. We discourage the pattern, do not reward it, and never schedule work that requires it.

## Not covered

- What to do when the supervisor is the source of the problem; the sources recommend second supervisors, codes of practice and departmental routes, and give no procedure for self-correction.
- Any threshold that turns an observation into a wellbeing flag. The sources give no numbers, so the windows the group monitor uses are Robert's settings and are stated as such.
- Institutional detail for KAUST: which office a student is referred to, and what a supervisor may and may not record about a personal matter under local policy. This must come from KAUST sources before any script relies on it.
- The evans2018 and woolston2019 articles were not fetched; both are cited here for their headline finding only and neither should carry a claim on its own.
- The lee2008-supervision framework is summarised from knowledge and not verified against the article; check it before a skill leans on the specific list of concepts.
- Supervision of a student who is co-supervised or based in another group, where the meeting rhythm and the record are shared with someone outside the group.
- Group meetings and cohort structures, which the sources recommend but which this file does not cover beyond noting them as a remedy for isolation.
