## What the evidence says

- Read the work before the meeting. Students expect the supervisor to have read the submitted draft, to be constructively critical, and to run the meeting as an exchange of ideas rather than a verdict (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].
- Comment on argument and structure before prose. The supervision handbooks sequence feedback on a draft that way, and treat teaching a student to write as part of supervision rather than a favour (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate, wisker-good-supervisor].
- Feedback that only tells is the common failure. Dialogic talk that asks the student to justify a choice or connect a result to the argument is what moves the work, while telling and status checking fill the time (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Do not solve the problem for the student. Ask open-ended questions such as "What do you think might be going wrong?" or "What would you try next?", and avoid micromanaging each step [crossley2025].
- Scaffold heavily at the start of a project and lighten later; targets and assignments between meetings are appropriate early and become unnecessary as independence grows [crossley2025].
- Speed is part of the message. Rapid feedback on requests, questions and drafts shows the work matters, and supervisors should protect calendar time to give it while it is still useful [maestre2019, crossley2025].
- Normalise setbacks explicitly. Make it clear that confusion, failed attempts and impostor feelings are common, admit what you do not know, and ask directly what the student is stuck on [crossley2025].
- Treat rejection as a normal event with a next step. Sharing your own rejections and discussing why a particular one happened reduces the pressure that follows every setback [maestre2019].
- Acknowledge small wins by name: a finished figure, a concept finally understood, a bug found. For a first-time researcher these are the moments that build momentum [crossley2025].
- Never compare one group member with another or with the supervisor as a student; comparison raises anxiety and lowers performance [maestre2019].
- Skill development is the primary responsibility, not the deliverable; say out loud which transferable skill a piece of work practised [crossley2025].
- Make the tacit explicit. What counts as a good draft, how much reading is enough, and what the supervisor will do with a draft are expectations the student cannot meet while they are unstated (from the book notes) [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Name the conceptual threshold. A student who looks stuck is often at a step change in understanding; naming it and scaffolding the crossing works better than asking for more output (from the book notes) [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Name the stage of the doctorate. Saying "this is the isolation stage after coursework" gives the student a vocabulary and a reason, where "you seem stuck" gives neither (from the book notes) [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd].

## Rules we adopt

1. Order feedback as argument, then evidence, then structure, then prose. Stop at the first level that needs work and say why the later levels were not reviewed (from [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], [wisker-good-supervisor]).
2. Every criticism names the artefact and the locator (file, page, figure, commit) so the student can find it without guessing (from [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd]).
3. Phrase the first response to a piece of work as a question about the student's reasoning, not as an instruction, unless the work is blocked on a fact the student cannot know (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [crossley2025]).
4. State at least one specific thing that worked, by name, whenever there is one. Generic praise does not count (from [crossley2025], [maestre2019]).
5. Give a turnaround commitment for anything you did not read, with a date, in the meeting record (from [maestre2019], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd]).
6. Every criticism carries a next action that fits inside the time until the next meeting (from [crossley2025]).
7. When a setback is discussed, name it as normal, give the next step, and where it helps name an equivalent setback of your own (from [maestre2019], [crossley2025]).
8. Never compare the student to another student, in speech or in the record. Compare only against their own plan and previous work (from [maestre2019]).
9. Name the skill being practised in each piece of feedback, so the record shows skill growth next to output (from [crossley2025]).
10. When a student appears stuck, name the likely threshold or stage before asking for output, and offer a smaller next step (from [wisker-good-supervisor], [phillips-pugh-how-to-get-a-phd]).
