# Peer review and revision

## What the evidence says

### What the response document is for

- The response to reviewers is submitted alongside the revised manuscript and
  summarizes the changes made in response to the critiques; authors too often
  spend their effort on the manuscript and leave the response unclear, which
  produces misunderstandings and can cost a good paper its acceptance
  [noble2017].
- Noble reports empirical evidence that papers which went through several
  rounds of review end up better cited than papers accepted quickly, and
  states that in nearly every case the manuscript improves through review
  [noble2017].
- The letter opens with an overview of the changes, naming the new data and
  new analyses that answer the most essential criticisms, and points out when
  several reviewers raised the same criticism; the full set of reviews then
  follows with the responses interleaved [noble2017].
- The response may contain figures and tables meant only for the reviewers
  and never entering the manuscript or its supplement [noble2017].

### Point-by-point structure

- Every point raised must get a response. A frequent reviewer complaint is
  that some points were not answered at all, and a difficult point must not
  be dodged by silence [noble2017].
- One bullet in a review often carries two separate issues; both get their own
  explicit response, and it is better to break the bullet apart than to answer
  several points in one block of text [noble2017].
- Each response begins with a direct answer to the point, ideally yes or no,
  with background afterwards; when the reviewer is right, say so [noble2017].
- Typeface, color and indentation separate three elements that the reviewer
  must be able to tell apart: the review, the response, and the change made to
  the manuscript. The conventions are explained at the top of the letter
  [noble2017].
- A point-by-point critique is what reviewers are asked to write because it is
  easy to read and easy to respond to, and each point should carry the
  reviewer's own judgement of how critical it is [bourne-korngreen2006].
- A good review addresses both major issues and minor details, so a response
  has to work at both levels [bourne-korngreen2006].

### The response is self-contained

- Quote the changed text or figure directly in the response, and give the line
  number where the change was applied, stating whether the numbers are those
  of the original or of the revised manuscript [noble2017].
- A self-contained letter spares the reviewer from moving back and forth
  between documents, and reduces the chance that a fresh reading of the whole
  manuscript turns up new complaints. The exception is a change too long to
  quote, such as a new section, which is named instead [noble2017].
- Saying that a point "is addressed in the manuscript" is a common error: it
  leaves the reviewer unable to tell whether the text was already there or is
  new. Refer explicitly to the previous and the revised version and say what
  changed [noble2017].

### Tone and disagreement

- Convey nothing about the reviewer's intellect. If the reviewer failed to
  understand something, the fault is at least partly the author's for not
  making it clear, and a reviewer who is not an expert stands in for many
  readers of the journal [noble2017].
- Off-base comments, especially many of them from one reviewer, are a signal
  that the manuscript does not explain its hypothesis well enough [noble2017].
- Accept the blame: apologize for what was unclear, and revise even text you
  consider already clear, quoting the revision, to show that the reviewer was
  heard [noble2017].
- A rude review does not license a rude response; the goal is publication of
  the results. Suspicion that a reviewer is a competitor acting in bad faith
  goes to the editor in a separate letter, never into the response [noble2017].
- Do what the reviewer asks where you can. Even for an analysis you consider
  uninformative, running it, reporting the result in the response, and then
  explaining why it does not belong in the manuscript is the stronger position
  [noble2017].
- Declaring most of a reviewer's requests out of scope does not work: Noble's
  example is nine of ten requests refused as out of scope, which will not
  satisfy the reviewer, and scope is rarely definable that precisely
  [noble2017].
- Conflicts between reviewers, or between a reviewer and journal policy, are
  put to the editor; a request to move supplementary content into the main text
  can be accepted subject to the editor's agreement [noble2017].
- Writing the response twice helps: a first draft that works out what the
  reviewer meant and what the cost of each option is, which is also where
  frustration can be vented, and then a separate document that the reviewers
  will actually see, written days later [noble2017].
- The correct answer to rejection or major revision is to listen and respond
  objectively rather than subjectively; unanimous reviewer agreement that the
  work is poor is a signal to move on rather than to argue
  [bourne2005].
- Objectivity about one's own work is what makes rejection bearable and the
  work better; a candid reading by colleagues without a stake in the paper is
  worth more than the mentor's opinion [bourne2005].

### What reviewers are told to do

- Reviewers are instructed to support every criticism with concrete, logical
  reasons, to write reviews they would be satisfied with as authors, and to
  highlight the strong points as well as the weak ones [bourne-korngreen2006].
- Reviewers should write in a neutral tone, be decisive, and give the editor a
  clear recommendation; fence-sitting prolongs the process
  [bourne-korngreen2006].
- Reviewers are told to include citations missed by the author, but not their
  own, and not to push their own hypotheses [bourne-korngreen2006].
- The reviewer is described as an unacknowledged part of the authoring process
  whose comments should produce a better paper, and a paper near rejection can
  be saved by several rounds of detailed revision [bourne-korngreen2006].
- Reviewers are told to decline assignments they cannot deliver on time or
  where a conflict of interest exists, to keep the manuscript confidential
  under the journal's anonymity policy, and to use the confidential comments
  to the editor for opinions their written review supports
  [bourne-korngreen2006].
- Zobel's refereeing guidance is compatible: read the whole paper, summarize
  it to show understanding, judge contribution, correctness, novelty and
  presentation, separate major from minor issues, be specific and
  constructive, and check that the references exist and say what is claimed
  [zobel-writing-for-computer-science].

### Claims, citations and the record

- Zobel's citation rules apply to text added during revision: cite only what
  you have read, never cite a source for a claim it does not make, cite the
  original source rather than a later paper repeating it, prefer archival
  sources to web pages, and check every reference against the original
  [zobel-writing-for-computer-science].
- Zobel treats confidentiality of manuscripts under review, authorship for
  intellectual contribution only, and duplicate publication as ethics
  questions, not matters of taste
  [zobel-writing-for-computer-science].
- The ICMJE recommendations cover responsibilities in the submission and peer
  review process, authorship and contributorship, corrections and version
  control, and disclosure of relationships and conflicts of interest, and as
  of the January 2026 update they carry separate sections on the use of
  artificial intelligence by authors, by reviewers, and the editor's role in
  ensuring responsible use [icmje].
- Under the ICMJE headings, changes made during revision that alter who
  contributed, or that correct the published record, are governed by the
  authorship and the corrections and version control sections rather than left
  to the authors' preference [icmje].

## Rules we adopt

1. Split the decision letter into individually numbered comments before
   writing anything, one comment per issue, breaking a bullet that carries two
   issues into two comments. Checked by `split_reviews.py`. (from [noble2017])
2. Every comment id gets a response. A response letter with an unanswered id
   is not submitted. Checked by `response_check.py`. (from [noble2017],
   [bourne-korngreen2006])
3. Open every response with a direct answer to the point, yes or no where the
   point admits one, and put the background after it. Robert enforces in
   review. (from [noble2017])
4. Quote the reviewer's comment verbatim above each response, and quote the
   new manuscript text in the response unless it is a whole new section, which
   is named instead. Verbatim quoting is checked by `response_check.py`. (from
   [noble2017])
5. State what changed and where: a section, figure, table or line reference,
   and whether the line numbers are those of the original or of the revised
   manuscript. Checked by `response_check.py`. (from [noble2017])
6. Never write that a point "is addressed in the manuscript" without saying
   whether the text is new. Every response either records a change or declares
   explicitly that no change was made, with the reason. Checked by
   `response_check.py`. (from [noble2017])
7. Keep a change log with one entry per manuscript change, its location, and
   the comment ids that prompted it. A response may not promise a change the
   log does not record. Checked by `response_check.py`. (from [noble2017])
8. Express disagreement as evidence plus a stated position: the result, the
   citation or the analysis you ran, then the conclusion. No dismissal without
   a reason, no complaint about the reviewer, no remark about the reviewer's
   competence or motives. Checked by `response_check.py`. (from [noble2017],
   [bourne2005], [bourne-korngreen2006])
9. Run the analysis the reviewer asked for when the cost is bearable, report
   the result in the response, and only then argue about whether it belongs in
   the manuscript. Robert decides when the cost is not bearable. (from
   [noble2017])
10. Refuse at most a small minority of requests as out of scope, and give a
    reason for each refusal. Many out-of-scope refusals in one letter mean the
    scope statement in the manuscript needs fixing instead. Robert enforces.
    (from [noble2017])
11. Concerns about reviewer conflicts of interest, reviewer misconduct,
    contradictions between reviewers, or conflicts with journal policy go in a
    separate letter to the editor, never in the response to reviewers. (from
    [noble2017], [bourne-korngreen2006])
12. Write the working draft first, with the cost and benefit of each option,
    and the letter the reviewers will see second, on a later day. The working
    draft stays local and is never submitted. (from [noble2017])
13. Every claim added to the manuscript during revision carries a citation to
    a source that has been read and that makes exactly that claim, with the
    reference checked against the original. Robert enforces in review. (from
    [zobel-writing-for-computer-science])
14. When revision changes who contributed (a new analysis run by someone else,
    a dropped section), settle authorship and contributor roles against the
    journal's policy before submitting, and record the decision. Robert
    decides. (from [icmje], [zobel-writing-for-computer-science])
15. Nothing in the revision cycle is submitted, uploaded or emailed by an
    agent. The prepared letter, manuscript and change log go to Robert as an
    approval item. (from [icmje])
16. When reviewers agree unanimously that the work is not sound, propose
    withdrawal or a redirected study rather than a rebuttal. Robert decides.
    (from [bourne2005])
17. When we referee, apply the same standard we ask of our reviewers: a
    point-by-point list, a reason for every criticism, major separated from
    minor, strong points named, a clear recommendation, and no request that
    the reviewer's own work be cited. (from [bourne-korngreen2006],
    [zobel-writing-for-computer-science])

## Where sources disagree

- Acknowledging reviewers: Noble notes that it can be appropriate to thank a
  reviewer in the acknowledgments for detailed suggestions, and that some
  authors do so routinely, while also noting that some journals, PLOS
  Computational Biology among them, forbid it [noble2017]. We follow the
  journal's policy and, absent a policy, thank reviewers collectively without
  naming them.
- How far to go in accepting blame: Noble advises apologizing and revising even
  where the author believes the text was already clear [noble2017], whereas
  Bourne asks for an objective rather than subjective response and treats
  unanimous negative judgement as a reason to stop [bourne2005]. We revise for
  clarity readily but do not concede a factual or methodological point we can
  support with evidence.
- Effort spent on weak submissions: as reviewers, Bourne and Korngreen advise
  spending little time on a poor paper while stating clearly why
  [bourne-korngreen2006]; Zobel's refereeing guidance asks for the whole paper
  to be read and summarized before judgement
  [zobel-writing-for-computer-science]. We read the whole paper before
  recommending rejection, then keep the report short.
- Reviewer anonymity: Bourne and Korngreen treat it as journal policy to be
  followed strictly, noting that opinion is divided [bourne-korngreen2006];
  the ICMJE recommendations govern it through the peer review responsibilities
  section [icmje]. We follow the venue's policy and never try to identify a
  reviewer.

## Not covered

- Response letters for conference venues with rebuttal word limits and no
  revised manuscript, where quoting changes is impossible; the sources assume
  a journal revision.
- Machine learning venue conventions (area chairs, reviewer scores, discussion
  periods, camera-ready page limits).
- Appeals against a rejection, and how to argue for a second round of review
  after a reject-and-resubmit decision.
- Public or open review, preprint comments, and post-publication review.
- Disclosure of the use of language models in preparing a revision beyond the
  fact that the ICMJE recommendations now carry sections on it; the fetched
  ICMJE page gives the section titles, so the detailed requirements must be
  read from the source before the skill relies on them.
- How to price the cost of a requested experiment against the chance of
  acceptance; the sources give no criterion.
