## What the evidence says

### Feasibility against interest, and the time it takes to choose

- A candidate problem can be placed on two axes: how feasible it is (how likely the work is to yield an answer with the time, skills and data at hand) and how much knowledge is gained if it does. The low-feasibility, low-gain quadrant is to be avoided; high-feasibility, low-gain problems are safe but forgettable; high-gain, low-feasibility problems suit experienced scientists with a track record; the target for a first project is high gain at feasible cost (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [alon2009].
- Feasibility is not a property of the problem alone; it depends on the group's data, tools and prior results, so the same question can be feasible in one lab and infeasible in another (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [alon2009].
- Choosing takes time. Students tend to seize the first idea offered; a period of about three months spent reading, talking and listing candidate problems before committing is a reasonable investment against a project that lasts years (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [alon2009].
- The expected path from question A to answer B is a schema. Real projects enter a "cloud" in which the plan fails and assumptions break, and they emerge at an unexpected answer C. The cloud is normal, not a sign of a bad problem; the group's job is to support the student while inside it (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [alon2009].
- A good problem is one the researcher can express themselves through: it should connect to the person's own curiosity and strengths, not only to what the lab needs done (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [alon2009].
- Gauge the novelty of a project and its potential for publications by reading the prior work and talking to scientists in related areas, and check whether the project "can be reasonably completed in the allocated time for graduation" [gu2007].
- A mentor who is not enthusiastic about the project signals the wrong project or the wrong mentor; equally, a mentor's enthusiasm can be misplaced relative to the student's interests [gu2007].
- Passion should drive the choice: research differs from coursework, and a student who feels no excitement about the selected project should not proceed with it [gu2007].

### Important problems and open doors

- Most scientists work on safe, incremental problems; the ones who do lasting work deliberately ask what the important problems in their field are and then work on one of them. Importance means that a solution would change what others can do, and an important problem must also have an attack available; a problem that matters but has no feasible approach is not a good choice at this time [hamming1986].
- Keeping the office door open costs interruptions but keeps the researcher aware of which problems matter; closed-door researchers finish more tasks and more often work on the wrong ones [hamming1986].
- Setting aside a fixed fraction of time, of the order of a tenth, for thinking about where the field is going and which problems are worth attacking produces better problem choices than reacting only to the task in front of you [hamming1986].
- Effort compounds: a small constant advantage in focus and hours yields a large gap over a career, so choosing to work on the right problem early matters more than raw speed [hamming1986].
- Preparation lets a researcher recognize an opportunity when a problem appears; a list of open problems kept in mind is what turns a chance observation into a project [hamming1986].
- Courage is part of choice: one has to be willing to work on a problem that might not yield, and to tolerate the ambiguity of believing in an approach enough to try it while doubting it enough to see when it fails [hamming1986].
- Work that is not communicated has little effect; a problem should be chosen with an eye to who will care about the answer and how they will learn of it [hamming1986].

### From topic to question to problem

- A topic ("protein function prediction") is not a project. A researchable question narrows the topic ("does adding phenotype similarity to a sequence model improve function prediction for orphan proteins?"), and a problem states why the answer matters to someone: what the reader cannot do or understand until the question is answered (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [booth-craft-of-research].
- The three-part formula "I am studying X, because I want to find out Y, in order to help my reader understand Z" forces the significance step; a candidate problem that cannot fill in Z fails the "so what" test (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [booth-craft-of-research].
- Conceptual problems (we do not understand something) and practical problems (something costs or harms) need different justifications; a computational biology project usually states a conceptual problem whose answer bears on a practical one, and conflating the two produces a proposal that promises the wrong thing (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [booth-craft-of-research].
- A successful study needs a narrow and clear research question; the way to find one is a systematic assessment of prior studies that identifies a gap that matters, and systematic reviews serve this better than opinion pieces [schwab2022].
- A vague question is refined through a structured frame; in clinical research the PICOT frame (population, intervention, comparator, outcome, time frame) forces the definition of population, outcome, data and sample size that follow from the question [schwab2022].
- Confirmatory and exploratory research differ in how precise the question must be at the start: exploratory omics work does not require a well-defined hypothesis, but it must be reported as exploratory rather than dressed as confirmation [schwab2022].
- Independence begins with the choice: students should define their own project as far as possible, with a view to a distinct contribution, rather than execute an assigned task [gu2007].

### The Heilmeier questions as a test of a candidate problem

- DARPA evaluates every proposed program against eight questions written by George Heilmeier, director from 1975 to 1977; the questions force the proposer to understand context, cost and effort before starting [heilmeier-catechism].
- The eight questions are: "What are you trying to do? Articulate your objectives using absolutely no jargon."; "How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?"; "What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful?"; "Who cares? If you are successful, what difference will it make?"; "What are the risks?"; "How much will it cost?"; "How long will it take?"; "What are the mid-term and final 'exams' to check for success?" [heilmeier-catechism].
- The first question tests whether the proposer can state the problem without hiding behind terminology; the second and third separate a real gap from a re-implementation; the fourth is the significance test; the last four turn a problem into a plan with risks, budget, duration and checkpoints [heilmeier-catechism].
- The "exams" question is the origin of the group's practice of writing success thresholds and kill criteria before the work starts: a problem for which no mid-term exam can be written is not yet a project [heilmeier-catechism].

### Problems that permit crucial experiments

- Strong inference is a loop: devise alternative hypotheses, devise a crucial experiment (or several) whose possible outcomes each exclude at least one hypothesis, carry out the experiment cleanly, and recycle with the hypotheses that remain. Fields that practiced this loop (molecular biology, high-energy physics in the 1950s and 1960s) moved faster than fields that did not (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [platt1964].
- The method of multiple working hypotheses protects the researcher from becoming attached to a single idea; a problem should be chosen so that at least two plausible explanations exist and an experiment can be designed to separate them (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [platt1964].
- The practical test of a candidate problem is the question "what experiment could disprove your hypothesis?" A project whose hypothesis no result could disprove is a survey, not an inference problem (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [platt1964].
- Pitfalls named in the essay include the eternal surveyor who collects data without a question, the never-finished project, and attachment to a favorite instrument or method; a logical tree written in a notebook before the work starts guards against all three (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [platt1964].
- Thinking is cheap compared with experiments; a short daily period of analytical thinking about which experiment would be decisive pays for itself many times (from knowledge of the source, not verified against the text) [platt1964].

### Registering the question, scoping, and the proposal document

- A study protocol written before the data are seen records the question, hypothesis, population, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria, design, data collection, processing and planned analyses; registering it reduces publication bias and hindsight bias and lets others compare what was done against what was planned [schwab2022].
- Registration does not remove flexibility: an analysis plan can be amended before data are observed, and exploratory analyses remain allowed as long as they are reported as such [schwab2022].
- Registered reports move peer review before the results are known, so the question and design are judged on their own merits [schwab2022].
- The scope of a thesis project should be defined early with the mentor and agreed as the set of work that earns the degree; the incentive for a supervisor to keep a productive student makes a written scope the student's protection [gu2007].
- Students lost in detail should return to the hypothesis and re-evaluate it periodically; a hypothesis that no longer makes sense should be replaced, in discussion with the mentor [gu2007].
- Setbacks are part of the choice: experiments fail, and experiments succeed without yielding anything useful; undergraduate training does not prepare students for this, so the problem should be scoped so that a failed sub-experiment is survivable [gu2007].
- KAUST PhD students write a research proposal that is defended before a committee; the CEMSE format is understood to be about ten pages, and it is the document in which question, significance, plan and checkpoints are fixed. The page count, required sections, timing relative to the qualifying exam, and committee composition are unverified until Robert confirms them against the current program guide [kaust-proposal-format].

## Rules we adopt

1. No student commits to a thesis problem in the first meeting. A choosing period of up to three months is planned, during which the student reads, talks to at least three people outside the immediate project, and maintains a written list of candidate problems (from [alon2009], [hamming1986]).
2. Every candidate problem is placed on the feasibility-against-interest diagram in writing, with one sentence for each axis stating why. The first project of a PhD is chosen from the feasible, high-gain region; low-gain problems are rejected regardless of feasibility (from [alon2009], [hamming1986]).
3. The chosen problem is written in the three-part form: "I am studying X, because I want to find out Y, in order to help Z understand or do W." A problem whose W is empty is sent back (from [booth-craft-of-research], [heilmeier-catechism]).
4. All eight Heilmeier questions are answered in plain language, each in at most three sentences, before the proposal is drafted. The answer to the first question contains no term that a biologist or computer scientist outside the subfield would need looked up (from [heilmeier-catechism]).
5. The problem statement names at least two competing hypotheses or approaches and at least one experiment whose outcome would exclude one of them. A problem with a single hypothesis and no disproving experiment is reframed or rejected (from [platt1964]).
6. The gap is established by a systematic search of prior work, recorded with the query and date, not by an opinion that the area is under-studied (from [schwab2022], [gu2007]).
7. The problem states whether the study is confirmatory or exploratory. Exploratory projects still name the space they explore and what pattern would count as a result (from [schwab2022]).
8. Feasibility is checked against the group's actual assets: which data are in hand, which are to be obtained and from where, which tools exist, and what compute is needed. A problem that depends on data not yet available lists the fallback if the data do not arrive (from [alon2009], [gu2007]).
9. The problem's fit with the student is recorded: what the student wants to learn and why this problem lets them express that. A mismatch is a reason to keep looking, not a detail to manage later (from [alon2009], [gu2007]).
10. Time to completion is estimated against the degree timeline before commitment, and the scope that earns the degree is written down and signed off by student and supervisor (from [gu2007], [alon2009]).
11. Mid-term and final "exams" are written for the chosen problem: what will be measured, by when, and what result means stop. These become the kill criteria of the research plan (from [heilmeier-catechism], [platt1964]).
12. The question, hypotheses, planned analyses and thresholds are frozen in a dated document before the main experiments run; later changes are recorded as amendments with a reason (from [schwab2022]).
13. The problem statement is re-read at every semester review; if the cloud has moved the project to a different answer, the statement is rewritten rather than left stale (from [alon2009], [gu2007]).
14. The ten-page proposal follows the CEMSE format once Robert has confirmed it; until then the group template uses the Heilmeier order (objective, current practice and limits, novelty, significance, risks, cost, timeline, checkpoints) (from [kaust-proposal-format], [heilmeier-catechism]).
15. Each student keeps at least one important open problem in view beside the thesis problem, and one meeting per semester is spent on where the field is going rather than on the current experiment (from [hamming1986]).

## Where sources disagree

- Ambition of the first problem: [hamming1986] urges working on important problems and warns that safe problems lead nowhere; [alon2009] places a beginner's first project in the feasible region and reserves hard, high-gain problems for later; [gu2007] asks for a project that can be completed within the time to graduation. We follow [alon2009] for the thesis problem and satisfy [hamming1986] through rule 15: the thesis problem is feasible and high gain, and an important open problem is kept in view for later work.
- Who chooses: [gu2007] wants the student to define the project as far as possible; [alon2009] describes choice as a joint act of student and mentor over months. We treat the choice as joint, with the student writing every version of the statement and the supervisor supplying the feasibility assessment of the group's assets.
- Fixed question versus evolving question: [schwab2022] asks for a registered protocol; [alon2009] and [gu2007] expect the question to change inside the cloud. We register, and we amend with dated reasons, which is what [schwab2022] itself allows.
- Exploratory work: [platt1964] treats data collection without a disproving experiment as a failure mode; [schwab2022] accepts exploratory research if labeled. We allow exploratory projects only when the statement says so and names the pattern that would count as a finding.

## Not covered

- No source gives evidence on how long a problem-choice period should be for computational projects; the three-month figure is a rule of thumb from [alon2009], recalled from knowledge and not verified.
- No source addresses how to choose a problem when the data are owned by a collaborator or a consortium, which is common in phenotype and variant work.
- The sources do not treat benchmark-driven fields, where the problem is partly fixed by a community challenge (CAFA, CAGI); how to choose between a challenge problem and a self-defined one is left to judgment.
- None of the sources says how to choose between a methods problem (a new ontology embedding) and an application problem (a new gene-disease prioritization) for a given student.
- The interaction between problem choice and funding constraints is not covered; the grant-writing file handles it.
