## What the evidence says

### The deficit model is the default error

- Lovitts found that doctoral completers and non-completers were not
  distinguishable by entering credentials such as test scores and prior
  grades; what differed was what happened after entry, above all contact with
  an advisor and integration into the department [lovitts2001].
- Departments and faculty nevertheless explain a departure by assuming the
  student lacked ability or commitment, students who leave often adopt the
  same explanation and blame themselves, and because departures are not
  tracked the pattern stays invisible. Lovitts calls the shared
  misperception pluralistic ignorance [lovitts2001].
- Structural factors that look like personal ones include having no desk in
  the group, holding a fellowship with no shared duties, an advisor chosen by
  default, and the unstructured move from coursework to research
  [lovitts2001].
- Some supervisors lose far more students than others, and departments rarely
  know because they keep no records; the pattern is only visible across cases
  and years [lovitts2001].
- Expectations that are tacit cannot be met, so a student who fails to produce
  the expected literature review, draft or report may never have been told
  what one looks like [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- A student who appears stuck is often crossing a conceptual threshold in how
  they understand the research, and the visible output lags the actual
  progress [wisker-good-supervisor].

### Judgement errors with names

- Bias is a systematic deviation of an estimate from the quantity it
  estimates; the more of it there is, the less valid the conclusion, which is
  why the risk of bias is assessed rather than asserted [schwab2022].
- Questionable practices are usually applied without bad intent, which is why
  naming them in advance works better than relying on good faith
  [schwab2022].
- Sutherland and colleagues' tips for interpreting claims name the errors that
  apply directly to reading a few weeks of a student's record: variation and
  chance produce differences on their own, no measurement is exact, small
  samples are unreliable, extreme values regress toward the mean, base rates
  must be considered before a signal is treated as informative, correlation
  does not establish causation, extrapolating beyond the observed range is
  risky, and a non-significant result is not evidence of no effect. The
  article was not fetched and this entry is from general knowledge
  [sutherland2013].
- The revised taxonomy insists that a cell of the table describes a task and
  not a person, so a level reached in one conversation is evidence about that
  answer on that day [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- The National Academies describe negative mentorship as its own phenomenon
  rather than an absence of good mentorship, and add that the mentee is the
  party least able to report it, so a supervisor's own account of the
  relationship is not neutral evidence [nap2019-mentorship].
- Culturally aware mentorship is treated as a specific competency, because
  identity affects access, belonging and the interpretation the supervisor
  puts on behaviour such as silence in meetings or reluctance to disagree
  [nap2019-mentorship].
- The graduate education report asks institutions to broaden what counts as a
  successful outcome beyond the academic path, since judging every student
  against the supervisor's own trajectory misreads their goals
  [nap2018-graduate-stem].
- Healthy-lab practice makes the same point at group level: every member is
  unique, the leader's job is to help each go as far as they can and want
  rather than as far as the leader wants, and evaluation is by the outcome of
  the work, not by time spent in the workplace [maestre2019].
- Crossley and Maini tailor a project to the student's background, goals and
  capacity, which means the same output from two students is not the same
  evidence [crossley2025].

### Structural checks that reduce it

- Monitor at the stage where the least value has been added, so a plan or a
  first result is reviewed early rather than a finished product late; this
  turns a late judgement about a person into an early correction of work
  [grove-high-output-management].
- Pair every indicator with a counter-indicator so speed is not bought with
  rework [grove-high-output-management].
- The amount of structure a person needs depends on their experience with the
  specific task rather than their seniority, so the level of direction is a
  decision about the task, not a rating of the person
  [grove-high-output-management].
- Record milestone outcomes, delays and departures for every student and
  review them across the group each year, so patterns rather than incidents
  drive conclusions [lovitts2001].
- Keep a written record of each supervision meeting with what was agreed and
  by when, which converts a later dispute about effort into a check against a
  dated record [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Reporting all findings, including those that did not work, is part of good
  practice; an evidence set built only from what went well is biased by
  construction [schwab2022].

## Rules we adopt

1. Structure before ability. Before any score below 3 stands, name the
   structural explanation that was checked and ruled out: meeting frequency,
   clarity of the plan, blocked data or compute, group integration, or the
   coursework-to-research transition. Run before the report is rendered.
   (from [lovitts2001], [nap2019-mentorship])
2. Never write a reason the student did not state. Health, motivation, family
   and personal circumstances never appear in a report, a bead or a note,
   whatever Robert suspects. (from [lovitts2001], [nap2019-mentorship])
3. Check recency. A score must be supported by evidence spanning the whole
   window, not the last week. If the evidence is concentrated in one week, say
   so in the report instead of scoring. (from [sutherland2013],
   [grove-high-output-management])
4. Check exchangeability. Ask whether the same evidence would produce the same
   score for a different student in the group. If not, the score is about the
   person and is dropped. (from [nap2019-mentorship], [maestre2019])
5. Check the base rate. Compare against what this project and this stage
   normally produce, not against the most productive student or against
   Robert's own past. A short window with few artefacts is weak evidence, not
   negative evidence. (from [sutherland2013], [crossley2025])
6. Score the work, never the person. Every note is about an artefact, a dated
   entry, or an answer given on a date, with the question recorded.
   (from [anderson-krathwohl2001])
7. Treat an apparent stall as a threshold hypothesis first: ask what the
   student is trying to work out before concluding the work has stopped.
   (from [wisker-good-supervisor])
8. Check what is missing from the evidence, since a student shows what worked;
   ask for failed runs and abandoned lines rather than inferring from their
   absence. (from [schwab2022])
9. Never compare two students in one report and never rank them. Patterns
   across the group are reviewed once a year, from milestone outcomes and
   delays, not inside an individual review.
   (from [nap2019-mentorship], [lovitts2001])
10. Read silence, deference and reluctance to disagree as features of a
    relationship with a power difference and a cultural context, never as
    evidence about ability or engagement. (from [nap2019-mentorship])
11. Judge against the student's stated goals in the IDP, not against an
    academic career path assumed by default.
    (from [nap2018-graduate-stem], [maestre2019])
12. When the evidence is thin, record `no evidence` and put the collection gap
    on Robert's side of the agenda. A missing record is never a low score.
    (from [schwab2022], [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate])

## Where sources disagree

- Whether to score at all: Lovitts's finding that ability does not separate
  completers from leavers argues against grading students [lovitts2001], while
  the programme requires semester evaluations and the group needs an early
  warning. We score practices and artefacts, keep the result away from the
  student, and require the structural check first.
- Individual versus structural attribution: the supervision handbooks put the
  supervisor's judgement of the student at the centre
  [wisker-good-supervisor, delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], while Lovitts
  and the National Academies locate most of the variance in structure
  [lovitts2001, nap2019-mentorship]. We take the structural view as the
  default hypothesis and require it to be excluded before any individual
  attribution is written down.
- Uniform standards versus tailored expectations: Crossley and Maini tailor
  scope to the individual [crossley2025], while exchangeability asks whether
  the same evidence would produce the same score for anyone
  [nap2019-mentorship]. We tailor the goals in the plan and keep the rubric
  descriptors uniform, so the tailoring is visible in the plan rather than
  hidden in the score.
- Measuring people at all: Grove's management view accepts indicators on
  people [grove-high-output-management]; the mentorship report warns that
  measurement distorts a relationship with a power difference
  [nap2019-mentorship]. We keep the indicators Robert-facing and require a
  counter-indicator for each.

## Not covered

- Whether these checks actually reduce bias. None of the sources evaluates a
  checklist of this kind, and the rules here are a house procedure.
- Gender, national origin and language bias in supervisory judgement
  specifically. The mentorship report treats identity in general terms and no
  fetched source measures it in a research group.
- How to correct a judgement already communicated to a committee or written
  into an official evaluation.
- What to do when the structural explanation is Robert's own availability,
  which the checks surface but no source tells us how to weigh.
- Sutherland and colleagues' article was not fetched; the list of errors here
  is from general knowledge and should be checked before it is quoted.
