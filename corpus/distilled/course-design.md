## What the evidence says

### Backward and integrated design

- Wiggins and McTighe plan in three stages: fix the desired results, decide
  what evidence would show they were reached, and only then plan the
  activities; planning from a textbook or from favourite activities is what
  they call activity-oriented and coverage-oriented design
  [wiggins-mctighe-ubd].
- Desired results are layered into enduring understandings, essential
  questions and the knowledge and skills needed to reach them; time is spent
  in proportion to whether something merits enduring understanding, is
  important to know, or is only worth being familiar with
  [wiggins-mctighe-ubd].
- Understanding has more than one facet (explanation, interpretation,
  application, perspective, empathy, self-knowledge); a student who can only
  recall has not shown understanding, so assessment asks for more than one
  facet and performance tasks are framed for a real goal, role, audience,
  situation, product and standard [wiggins-mctighe-ubd].
- Fink's integrated course design has four components that must fit each
  other: situational factors, learning goals, feedback and assessment, and
  teaching and learning activities; breaking one connection inevitably
  breaks another, and with two broken connections the course is broken
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013].
- Fink's design process runs in twelve steps and three phases: situational
  factors, goals, assessment, activities and an integration check; then
  course structure, instructional strategy and the week-by-week scheme; then
  grading, debugging, syllabus and course evaluation; later steps build on
  earlier ones, so each is finished before the next
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Situational factors to review before setting goals: class size and level,
  meeting pattern and delivery, expectations of the department, profession
  and society, the nature of the subject (theoretical or practical,
  convergent or divergent, contested), the learners' prior knowledge and
  life situation, and the teacher's own beliefs and strengths
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013].
- Fink asks what should distinguish students two or three years after the
  course from those who never took it; content-centred goal lists produce
  mostly "understand and remember" goals, and his taxonomy of significant
  learning adds application, integration, human dimension, caring and
  learning how to learn, which reinforce one another and are not ranked
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013].
- Fink's integration worksheet lists each goal with the assessment that
  would show it was reached and the activity that leads to it; the exercise
  exposes goals that only get lip service [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- A course design is good when it rests on an analysis of situational
  factors, has goals in several kinds of significant learning, uses
  educative assessment, uses active learning, and has all components aligned
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Wilson's book on teaching technical subjects applies the same order to
  lessons: write learner personas, draw a concept map, write the summative
  and formative exercises, and only then write the teaching material
  [wilson-teaching-tech-together].

### Constructive alignment and outcome verbs

- Biggs's constructive alignment joins constructivism (students build
  meaning through what they do) with alignment of intended learning outcomes,
  teaching and learning activities and assessment tasks around the same
  verbs; students learn what they expect to be assessed on, so the
  assessment must demand the intended learning [biggs1996,
  biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning].
- An intended learning outcome has a verb at a chosen level of
  understanding, the content the verb acts on, and the context or standard;
  the verb is the hinge, and the assessment must ask for the same verb
  [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning].
- The SOLO taxonomy grades responses from unistructural through
  multistructural and relational to extended abstract; mapping verbs to
  SOLO levels lets outcomes and tasks be compared for level
  [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning].
- Surface and deep approaches are responses to the teaching environment:
  recall-rewarding assessment, heavy workload and unclear goals push
  students to surface learning; aligned tasks that need understanding pull
  them to deep learning [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning].
- The revised Bloom taxonomy makes an objective a verb plus a noun: the
  cognitive process (remember, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, create)
  and the knowledge type (factual, conceptual, procedural, metacognitive);
  objectives, activities and assessments should sit in the same cell, and
  teaching procedures while assessing recall is the commonest fault
  [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- The Bloom hierarchy is loose: categories overlap and a person can
  evaluate without first applying; retention needs remembering, transfer
  needs the other five; a cell describes a task, not a person
  [anderson-krathwohl2001].
- Garcia and colleagues advise that learning outcomes be written "using
  active verbs that express the expected behaviors of trainees" and be
  published with prerequisites and target audience so learners can judge
  fit [garcia2020].
- Via and colleagues put the objectives on a slide at the start and also
  state what participants will not learn, to keep expectations realistic
  [via2011].

### Assessment that teaches

- Fink distinguishes audit-ive assessment (only for a grade) from educative
  assessment with four components: forward-looking tasks set in the
  situation where the knowledge will be used, published criteria and
  standards, student self-assessment, and feedback that is frequent,
  immediate, discriminating and delivered with care
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013].
- The formative feedback principles Fink reproduces include clarifying what
  good performance is, giving low-stakes tasks with feedback before
  high-stakes graded ones, allowing resubmission, and releasing grades on
  written work only after students have responded to comments
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Biggs and Tang argue for criterion-referenced grading against the outcome
  standard with rubrics that describe levels of attainment, not marks summed
  across unrelated items and not rank in the class
  [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning].
- Nilson's specifications grading replaces partial credit with pass or fail
  against a published specification for each assignment, set at about
  B-level work; assignments are grouped into bundles tied to outcomes, a
  harder bundle earns a higher grade, and a few tokens replace ad hoc
  extensions and allow one revision [nilson-specifications-grading].
- Specifications double as one-level rubrics written as checklists that a
  student can check before submitting, and grading time falls because the
  judgement is yes or no against the spec [nilson-specifications-grading].
- Angelo and Cross describe classroom assessment techniques: short,
  ungraded, usually anonymous checks such as the minute paper, the muddiest
  point, a background knowledge probe or a one-sentence summary, run in a
  plan, implement and respond cycle so the teacher adjusts the next class
  [angelo-cross-classroom-assessment].
- Via and colleagues suggest a brief set of questions before and after a
  course to check that it was pitched correctly and to make the gain visible
  to trainees [via2011].
- Peer instruction questions should be pitched so that roughly 40 to 60
  percent answer correctly at first and every wrong option corresponds to a
  misconception, a design rule the Reaching Students report also gives
  [nap2015-reaching-students].

### Activities, structure and the week plan

- Active learning in Fink's holistic sense combines three components,
  getting information and ideas, experience (doing or observing, real or
  simulated) and reflective dialogue with oneself or others; a full set of
  activities covers all three and prefers direct forms over vicarious ones
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Moving first exposure to content out of class (readings, a course site,
  short videos) frees class time for experience and reflection
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Course structure means four to seven major topics in a sequence, with
  assignments that grow in complexity as topics accumulate; an instructional
  strategy sequences in-class and out-of-class activities so that energy
  builds, and the two together give the week-by-week schedule with a
  varied pattern within each one to three week block
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013].
- Via and colleagues plan a course as independent modules, each with an
  introduction, aims, actions and likely difficulties; each module recalls
  the previous one's achievements and ends with 10 to 15 minutes of review;
  they warn against spending most of a session talking and then rushing the
  practical, and against telling trainees to finish on their own [via2011].
- Examples should carry biological context relevant to at least some of
  the audience; a worked example on a common dataset comes first, then
  exploration on a second dataset or the learners' own data [via2011].
- Pavelin and colleagues reserve interactive workshops for exploring a
  problem once goals exist; they need two to three hours, facilitators who
  coordinate but do not contribute ideas, a hands-on activity as early as
  possible, a balance of individual, paired and collective tasks, and time
  to present back after every activity [pavelin2014].
- Discipline-based education research finds that students arrive with
  persistent misconceptions, that active engagement and interaction improve
  conceptual understanding, and that the evidence base is largely from
  undergraduate science and engineering [nap2012-dber].
- The Reaching Students report translates that research into practice:
  set learning goals, use pre-class preparation, structure class around
  problems and peer discussion, and use frequent low-stakes assessment to
  see what students think [nap2015-reaching-students].

### Grading and workload

- The grading system reflects the full range of goals, but not everything
  has to be graded, and the weight of each component should mirror its
  importance [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Debugging the design means checking whether students have the time for
  the out-of-class work and access to the resources it needs before the
  semester starts [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Wiggins and McTighe check the learning plan with the WHERETO questions:
  students know where the unit is going and why, are hooked, are equipped,
  can rethink and revise, can evaluate their own work, are taught to their
  needs, and the sequence is organised for engagement [wiggins-mctighe-ubd].

### Syllabus and evaluation

- Fink's syllabus contains management information, the goals, the structure
  and sequence with due dates, the required materials, the grading
  procedures and the policies (attendance, honour code, late work, make-ups)
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Course evaluation is planned as part of the design and collects
  information during the semester as well as at the end, from student
  ratings, interviews or questionnaires, outside observers and test
  results, to answer specific questions about whether the goals were met
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide].
- Fink advises first-order changes: on a first redesign add one kind of
  significant learning, one kind of educative assessment and one kind of
  active learning, then check alignment, and make more ambitious changes
  the next time the course runs [fink2003-self-directed-guide].

### FAIR and accessible materials

- Garcia and colleagues' rules for FAIR training materials: plan to share
  online, describe with structured metadata (Bioschemas TrainingMaterial,
  EDAM keywords), give a persistent identifier, register in a training
  registry, state access rules, use an interoperable and editable format,
  license for reuse and say how to cite, publish learning outcomes and
  prerequisites for learners, invite contributions, and keep materials
  dated and up to date [garcia2020].
- Their suggested metadata: title, contact, license and reuse conditions,
  preferred citation, description, learning outcomes, target audience and
  prerequisites, required resources, keywords, structure and duration,
  links and date of last revision [garcia2020].
- On formats, they observe that PDF is portable but hard to edit, so the
  source files (LaTeX or PPTX) should be shared alongside; Markdown and
  LaTeX are version-controlled and free but have a learning curve
  [garcia2020].
- Via and colleagues avoid screenshots because they date quickly and point
  learners at official sites as stable references [via2011].
- The UDL guidelines ask for multiple means of engagement (choice,
  relevance, clear goals, collaboration, action-oriented feedback),
  representation (customisable display, clarified vocabulary and notation,
  multiple media, links to prior knowledge) and action and expression
  (multiple media and tools for response, graduated support, goal setting
  and progress monitoring) [cast-udl].

### KAUST rules that shape a course

- The program guide states that a PhD needs at least 72 credits and an MS at
  least 36, a cumulative GPA of at least 3.0 to graduate, and that
  "Individual courses require a minimum of a B- for course credit"
  [kaust-registrar-program-guide].
- Core and elective courses listed in the CS program carry 3 credits each;
  core courses may be offered only once per academic year, so a course that
  is core for a program has a fixed semester [kaust-registrar-program-guide].
- PhD students must take at least two 300-level courses (6 credits), and
  the CS qualifier is course-based: three courses from three different
  faculty, one at A- or A and two at B+ or better, so grades in a 300-level
  course can decide a qualifier [kaust-registrar-program-guide].
- Some courses (the graduate seminar, the introductory programming
  courses for CS students) are graded satisfactory or unsatisfactory and do
  not count toward degree credit; MS electives must be at the 200 or 300
  level [kaust-registrar-program-guide].
- The academic year has Semesters and Summer Sessions; the program guide's
  policy pages on grading, add and drop, attendance, audit and academic
  integrity exist but were not fetched [kaust-registrar-program-guide].

## Rules we adopt

1. Design in this order and record each step in `course.yaml`: situational
   factors, outcomes, assessments, activities, alignment check, week plan,
   grading, debugging, syllabus, evaluation plan. No lecture material is
   written before the alignment check passes. Checked by the course-design
   procedure. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide], [wiggins-mctighe-ubd])
2. Every outcome is a verb plus a noun plus a context, carries a Bloom
   level and a knowledge type, and is tagged with the kind of significant
   learning it serves. A course whose outcomes are all remember or
   understand is flagged. Checked by `alignment_matrix.py`. (from
   [anderson-krathwohl2001], [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning],
   [fink2003-self-directed-guide])
3. Every outcome is tested by at least one assessment and practised by at
   least one activity; an assessment that tests no declared outcome is an
   error. `alignment_matrix.py` exits 2 on an unassessed outcome or an
   orphan assessment. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide], [biggs1996],
   [wiggins-mctighe-ubd])
4. Activities and assessments carry the level of the verb they make
   students perform; an activity below the level of an outcome it serves is
   an error, and an assessment below the level of an outcome it tests is a
   warning. Checked by `alignment_matrix.py`. (from [anderson-krathwohl2001],
   [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning])
5. Grade weights sum to 100 and mirror the importance of the outcomes; not
   every activity is graded. `alignment_matrix.py` exits 2 when weights do
   not sum to 100. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide])
6. Every graded task has a published criterion: a specification checklist
   for projects, labs and reports (pass or fail, B-level bar, tokens for
   late work and one revision) and a rubric with level descriptors where
   points are unavoidable, such as exams. The bundle-to-letter mapping is in
   the syllabus. (from [nilson-specifications-grading],
   [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning])
7. Practice with feedback precedes every graded task: a low-stakes version
   of each assessment type appears in the week plan before the graded one.
   `alignment_matrix.py` warns when an assessment is due before any
   activity that serves its outcomes. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide])
8. Assessments are forward-looking where the subject allows: tasks set in
   the situation a researcher or engineer would meet, framed with goal,
   role, audience, situation, product and standard. (from
   [fink2003-self-directed-guide], [wiggins-mctighe-ubd])
9. Structure the semester as four to seven major topics with assignments
   that grow in complexity; every one to three week block mixes information,
   experience and reflection, and first exposure to content happens out of
   class. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide], [via2011])
10. Every module ends with a review and a classroom assessment technique
    (minute paper or muddiest point); the course collects a background
    probe in week one, a mid-semester check and an end-of-semester
    evaluation, and the results feed the next iteration's first-order
    changes. (from [angelo-cross-classroom-assessment], [via2011],
    [fink2003-self-directed-guide])
11. The syllabus states what the course does not cover, and the outcomes,
    prerequisites and target audience appear in the syllabus and in the
    material's metadata. `syllabus_build.py` renders these from
    `course.yaml`. (from [via2011], [garcia2020])
12. Course materials are FAIR by default: an editable source format under
    version control, a CC-BY license, a citation, a dated revision, and a
    persistent identifier on release; PDFs ship with their sources.
    Student work and anything about individual students is never part of
    the shared materials. (from [garcia2020])
13. Materials follow the UDL guidelines: every figure has text, notation is
    defined when introduced, and students have more than one way to show
    an outcome where the outcome allows it. (from [cast-udl])
14. The syllabus is KAUST-shaped: code, credits and level, semester,
    meeting pattern, instructor and office hours, prerequisites, outcomes,
    assessment table with weights and due weeks, grading scale with the
    bundle mapping, week plan, materials, policies (attendance, integrity,
    late work, accommodation, AI use) and the evaluation plan; it notes
    that a B- is the minimum for course credit and, for 300-level courses,
    that grades can count toward the CS qualifier. Rendered by
    `syllabus_build.py`. (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide],
    [kaust-registrar-program-guide])
15. Before the semester, debug the design: total out-of-class hours per
    week, cluster access and software installation for labs, dataset
    availability, and a fallback for every live-service dependency. (from
    [fink2003-self-directed-guide], [via2011])
16. A course redesign changes a few things per iteration and states them in
    the evaluation plan; wholesale rewrites are not attempted mid-semester.
    (from [fink2003-self-directed-guide])

## Where sources disagree

- Hierarchy of goals: Fink's taxonomy of significant learning is
  deliberately non-hierarchical [fink2003-self-directed-guide, fink2013],
  while the revised Bloom taxonomy orders cognitive processes
  [anderson-krathwohl2001] and SOLO orders response quality
  [biggs-tang-teaching-for-quality-learning]. We use Bloom levels for the
  mechanical level check because they are comparable across outcomes,
  activities and assessments, and use Fink's kinds as a completeness
  checklist for the outcome list.
- Pass or fail versus points: Nilson grades each task pass or fail against
  a specification [nilson-specifications-grading]; KAUST records letter
  grades with a B- minimum for credit and grade thresholds in the CS
  qualifier [kaust-registrar-program-guide]. We use specifications for
  project-style work and publish which bundle maps to which letter grade,
  and keep points with rubrics for exams.
- Context in tasks: Wiggins and McTighe and Fink want tasks set in
  authentic situations [wiggins-mctighe-ubd, fink2003-self-directed-guide];
  the Reaching Students literature reports that problem context does not
  change performance on its own [nap2015-reaching-students]. We use
  authentic context for motivation and transfer, not as a substitute for
  aligned verbs.
- Granularity of structure: Fink wants four to seven topics per course
  [fink2003-self-directed-guide]; Via wants independent modules per session
  [via2011]. These are two levels of one hierarchy: topics span weeks,
  modules span sessions, and the week plan carries both.
- Openness: Garcia and colleagues ask that materials be shared online
  [garcia2020]; KAUST privacy rules and this repo's privacy classes keep
  anything about students local. We share instructor materials openly and
  never student work, grades or class recordings.
- Evidence base: the DBER and Reaching Students reports draw on
  undergraduate STEM [nap2012-dber, nap2015-reaching-students]; our courses
  are graduate. We apply the same design rules and treat effect sizes as
  indicative, not guaranteed.
