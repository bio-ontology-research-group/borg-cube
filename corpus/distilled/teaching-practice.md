## What the evidence says

### Active learning beats lecturing

- Freeman and colleagues' meta-analysis of 225 studies in undergraduate
  science, engineering and mathematics found that active learning raised
  examination performance by about half a standard deviation and that
  failure rates under traditional lecturing were about 34 percent against
  about 22 percent with active learning [freeman2014].
- Wieman's commentary on that analysis argues that the effect is large
  enough that lecturing is no longer a defensible control condition and
  that the question is how to implement active learning, not whether
  [wieman2014].
- Theobald and colleagues' meta-analysis found that active learning
  narrowed achievement gaps for underrepresented students in exam scores
  and in passing rates, and that the narrowing was largest when active
  learning filled most of class time and was paired with an inclusive
  classroom culture [theobald2020].
- Deslauriers and colleagues compared identical physics content taught by a
  polished lecture and by active learning: students in the active section
  learned more on a test but reported feeling that they learned less, and
  the authors recommend telling students early why active methods feel
  harder and work better [deslauriers2019].
- Crouch and Mazur's ten years of peer instruction at Harvard report
  learning gains on concept inventories roughly double those of the
  lecture years, with pre-class reading and short reading quizzes
  making the in-class questions work [crouch-mazur2001].
- The Reaching Students report packages the research for instructors: set
  goals, have students prepare before class, spend class time on problems
  and peer discussion, and check understanding often
  [nap2015-reaching-students].

### Peer instruction and prediction

- Peer instruction runs in phases: a short introduction, a multiple choice
  question that probes a misconception, an individual vote, a few minutes
  of small-group discussion, a second vote, and the instructor acting on
  the result; "The ideal questions are those for which 40%–60% of students
  are likely to get the right answer the first time" [brown-wilson2018].
- Every wrong option should correspond to a misconception that some
  students will pick; a question everyone gets right or whose wrong options
  nobody chooses teaches nothing [brown-wilson2018, nap2015-reaching-students].
- Demonstrations by themselves do not improve learning and are often
  misremembered; asking learners to commit to a prediction in public before
  the demonstration is what makes it work, and wrong predictions are used
  as a spur, never punished [brown-wilson2018].

### Live coding, worked examples and pairs

- Live coding instead of slides lets the instructor follow "what if"
  questions, transfers tacit knowledge about how experts work, slows the
  instructor to a pace learners can follow, shows how mistakes are
  diagnosed, and makes mistakes acceptable [brown-wilson2018].
- Boilerplate is prepared in advance as a skeleton so that typing it does
  not become what learners remember; reference material is available after
  the session [brown-wilson2018].
- Worked examples broken into named subgoals help novices see that
  problems are isomorphic and give them a vocabulary for asking questions
  [brown-wilson2018].
- Pair programming with a driver and a navigator who swap roles three or
  four times an hour helps both partners; everyone is paired so nobody is
  singled out [brown-wilson2018].
- Courses stick to one language until learners can tell the essential
  from the accidental, because transfer follows mastery [brown-wilson2018].
- Authentic tasks (real data, real media) improve retention and persistence;
  context makes no measurable difference to performance, so it is chosen
  for motivation, with care that it does not exclude anyone
  [brown-wilson2018].
- Novices are not slow experts: they need small self-contained tasks,
  starter code that puts them close to a visible result, and exercises such
  as Parsons problems that isolate one concept [brown-wilson2018].
- Competence at programming is learned, not innate; instructors who believe
  in a "geek gene" invest less in the students they have written off
  [brown-wilson2018].

### Formative assessment during class

- The Carpentries instructor training builds each session around frequent
  formative assessment: a multiple choice or short exercise every ten to
  fifteen minutes so both learner and instructor find out what has been
  understood while there is time to act on it
  [carpentries-instructor-training].
- Sticky notes in two colours (done, or stuck) and minute cards at each
  break (one thing learned, one question) give the instructor a continuous
  read of the room without stopping the class
  [carpentries-instructor-training].
- Angelo and Cross's classroom assessment techniques are short, ungraded
  and usually anonymous: the minute paper, the muddiest point, the
  background knowledge probe, the one-sentence summary, application cards;
  the value is in the response the teacher makes at the next class
  [angelo-cross-classroom-assessment].
- Via and colleagues confirm that everyone reached the result after each
  practical task, recapitulate what was done and why, and schedule 10 to 15
  minutes of review and questions at the end of each module [via2011].
- Nilson's handbook recommends breaking any lecture into segments of
  roughly ten to fifteen minutes separated by an activity, because
  attention and retention fall after that, and pairing every lecture
  segment with a question students answer [nilson-teaching-at-its-best].

### Motivation, climate and inclusion

- The Carpentries name demotivators to avoid: telling learners something
  is easy, dismissive language, letting one learner dominate, and pace set
  by the fastest; and motivators to use: authentic tasks, early wins, and
  showing where a skill is used in real work [carpentries-instructor-training].
- The Carpentries teach expertise as a mental model: experts have densely
  connected knowledge and blind spots about what novices find hard, so
  instructors surface learners' mental models and address misconceptions
  rather than only adding facts [carpentries-instructor-training].
- Wilson's book treats teaching as a performance to be rehearsed and
  reviewed: instructors practise in front of peers, get feedback with a
  rubric, and keep a teaching diary; a lesson is designed for a few written
  learner personas rather than for an imagined average student
  [wilson-teaching-tech-together].
- Wilson recommends writing the exercises before the material, keeping
  each lesson to a handful of concepts drawn on a concept map, and pacing
  by the learners' formative results rather than by the slides
  [wilson-teaching-tech-together].
- Theobald and colleagues attribute part of the gap narrowing to a
  classroom culture that values participation from everyone [theobald2020].
- The UDL guidelines ask instructors to offer choice and relevance, to
  clarify the purpose of goals, to foster collaboration and belonging, and
  to give action-oriented feedback [cast-udl].
- Brown and Wilson close with the human element: gatekeeping and scorn for
  a learner's existing tools make people feel they cannot succeed, and the
  goal is for learners to feel they can be programmers [brown-wilson2018].

### Running practicals and workshops

- Via and colleagues insist on prepared workstations with one computer per
  trainee or pair, a support person in the room at the start, tested
  exercises, a backup plan when live services fail, and enough helpers for
  the range of backgrounds [via2011].
- Trainees of different backgrounds are paired, activities move at a pace
  all can follow, and slide-heavy sessions with a rushed practical at the
  end are avoided [via2011].
- Pavelin and colleagues run interactive workshops with a hands-on activity
  as early as possible, few slides, individual silent brainstorming before
  group sharing so everyone contributes, briefed facilitators who stay
  impartial, and time to present back after each activity [pavelin2014].
- Workshop artefacts are photographed on the day, participants introduce
  themselves before sharing ideas, and a report goes out soon after to keep
  momentum [pavelin2014].

## Rules we adopt

1. No session is a continuous lecture: plan an interaction (peer
   instruction question, prediction, minute paper, exercise) at least every
   fifteen minutes, and record it in the session plan. Checked by the
   lecture-design skill. (from [freeman2014], [nilson-teaching-at-its-best],
   [carpentries-instructor-training])
2. Explain in week one, and again when students complain, why active
   methods feel harder and produce more learning, quoting the evidence.
   (from [deslauriers2019])
3. Peer instruction questions probe a named misconception, have wrong
   options that some students will choose, and are pitched so that roughly
   half the class gets them right the first time; questions everybody gets
   right are removed. Checked by the lecture-design skill. (from
   [brown-wilson2018], [nap2015-reaching-students], [crouch-mazur2001])
4. Every demonstration, live run or plotted result is preceded by a
   recorded prediction (show of hands, cards, poll). (from
   [brown-wilson2018])
5. Code is taught by live coding from a prepared skeleton, with the final
   code published after the session; slides of code are not used. (from
   [brown-wilson2018])
6. Worked examples are written with labelled subgoals; practicals use pairs
   with driver and navigator roles that swap at least three times per hour,
   and everyone is paired. (from [brown-wilson2018])
7. Every class or module ends with a classroom assessment technique
   (minute paper or muddiest point) and the next class opens by answering
   what it revealed. (from [angelo-cross-classroom-assessment], [via2011])
8. Each practical is tested end to end on the target machines before the
   session, has one computer per student or pair, a helper ratio suited to
   the range of backgrounds, and a fallback when a service is down. (from
   [via2011])
9. Pre-class preparation (reading, video, warm-up question) is assigned for
   every session that builds on it, and a short reading check makes it
   count. (from [crouch-mazur2001], [nap2015-reaching-students])
10. Never say a task is easy; never let one learner set the pace; pair
    learners of different backgrounds; pick examples that draw people in
    rather than out. (from [carpentries-instructor-training],
    [brown-wilson2018], [via2011])
11. Rehearse a new session in front of a peer or a recording, review it
    against a rubric, and keep a dated teaching diary of what to change.
    (from [wilson-teaching-tech-together], [carpentries-instructor-training])
12. Group idea-generation sessions (project scoping, design critiques) use
    silent individual writing before discussion, briefed facilitators and a
    present-back step, and their artefacts are photographed and filed the
    same day. (from [pavelin2014])

## Where sources disagree

- Slides: Via and colleagues assume slide-based sessions and ask for an
  objectives slide [via2011]; Brown and Wilson replace slides with live
  coding for programming [brown-wilson2018]; Pavelin and colleagues keep
  electronic presentations to a minimum in workshops [pavelin2014]. We use
  slides for concepts and framing, live coding for code, and none for
  brainstorming.
- Context in exercises: Brown and Wilson report that problem context does
  not change performance and recommend choosing it for motivation
  [brown-wilson2018]; Via and colleagues hold that examples without
  relevant context fail to engage [via2011]. Both point to motivation, so
  we pick contexts relevant to the audience and never rely on context to
  carry an unaligned task.
- Feeling versus learning: Deslauriers and colleagues show that students
  rate active classes lower while learning more [deslauriers2019]; student
  ratings are part of KAUST's course evaluation. We collect ratings, read
  them alongside the assessment evidence, and pre-empt the effect by
  explaining the method in week one.
- Evidence base: Freeman, Theobald, Crouch and Mazur and the Reaching
  Students report are undergraduate STEM [freeman2014, theobald2020,
  crouch-mazur2001, nap2015-reaching-students]; the Carpentries and Brown
  and Wilson address short workshops and programming
  [carpentries-instructor-training, brown-wilson2018]. We apply the rules to
  graduate courses and record in the course evaluation whether they held.

## Not covered

- Graduate seminars, reading groups and research-project courses; none of
  the sources studies them.
- Assessment of oral contributions and participation grades.
- Teaching assistants: training, briefing and grading consistency are not
  addressed beyond the helper ratio for practicals.
- Online and hybrid delivery beyond Pavelin's observation that interactive
  workshops do not work remotely.
- Freeman 2014, Deslauriers 2019, Theobald 2020, Wieman 2014, Crouch and
  Mazur 2001, the Reaching Students report, Nilson's handbook and Angelo and
  Cross were not fetched; the numbers and claims here come from memory and
