## What the evidence says

### Mentorship is a distinct relationship with two functions

- The National Academies treat mentorship as a professional, reciprocal
  relationship that supports two things at once: career development (skills,
  projects, sponsorship, networks) and psychosocial development (confidence,
  belonging, identity as a scientist). Advising, supervising and managing are
  related but narrower roles, and one person holding all of them is a common
  source of strain [nap2019-mentorship].
- The report's summary of the literature reports associations between
  effective mentorship and persistence in the field, productivity,
  self-efficacy and a sense of belonging, and reports that most of the
  underlying studies are correlational and self-reported rather than
  experimental [nap2019-mentorship].
- Negative mentorship is a separate phenomenon and not simply the absence of
  good mentorship: neglect, over-control, misappropriated credit and
  exploitation have their own effects, and they are under-measured because
  the mentee is the party with least power to report them
  [nap2019-mentorship].
- The graduate education report frames the supervisor relationship as the
  main determinant of a student's experience and asks institutions to treat
  mentoring as a competency to be developed and reviewed rather than a
  personal trait [nap2018-graduate-stem].

### Mentoring is trainable, and training changes behavior

- Entering Mentoring was written as a facilitator's manual for a seminar in
  which graduate students and postdocs learn to mentor while they are
  mentoring, using case studies and structured discussion rather than
  lectures; its premise is that mentoring is a laboratory skill that is
  normally never taught, so untrained mentors reproduce what happened to them
  [handelsman2005].
- Its themes are the components a supervisor's practice can be checked
  against: establishing expectations, communication, assessing understanding,
  addressing diversity, fostering independence, ethics, and writing a
  personal mentoring philosophy [handelsman2005].
- The evaluation compared trained and untrained mentors of undergraduates and
  reported that trained mentors discussed expectations more often, thought
  more about diversity in the relationship, and were more confident. The
  result comes from the book notes and the study itself was not fetched
  [pfund2006, handelsman2005].
- CIMER now distributes the curriculum family that grew out of that seminar:
  Entering Mentoring, Entering Research, Mentoring Up and Culturally Aware
  Mentoring, with facilitator training behind each [cimer-entering-mentoring].
- Nature's guide for mentors describes the qualities that mentees name in
  good mentors, including availability, enthusiasm, respect, appreciation of
  individual differences, and willingness to sponsor a mentee's career beyond
  the mentor's own interest, and separates the roles of adviser, coach,
  sponsor and role model. This entry is from general knowledge; the article
  was not fetched [lee2007-nature-mentors].

### One mentor is not enough

- The National Academies describe mentorship as a network rather than a dyad:
  different needs (technical method, career outside academia, culture,
  wellbeing) are best met by different people, and a mentee with one mentor
  carries all the risk of that single relationship [nap2019-mentorship].
- Culturally aware mentorship is treated as a specific competency: mentors who
  can discuss identity, and who recognise how it affects access and belonging,
  are associated with better outcomes for students from groups
  under-represented in the field [nap2019-mentorship].
- Structured programmes make matching an explicit, monitored process rather
  than a one-time pairing: match criteria are decided in advance, and the
  match is monitored and can be changed [pcbi-2022-mentorship-programme].
- A programme is expected to plan its activities, its communication routes and
  its monitoring and evaluation in advance, so that the effect of the
  programme can be shown rather than assumed
  [pcbi-2022-mentorship-programme].
- The HHMI and Burroughs Wellcome guide advises new group leaders to set up a
  mentoring structure for their own people, and to seek several mentors for
  themselves, rather than relying on one senior colleague. This entry is from
  general knowledge; the guide was not fetched
  [hhmi-bwf-making-the-right-moves].

### Structure, not ability, explains most departures

- Lovitts found that doctoral completers and non-completers were not
  distinguishable by entering credentials; what differed was what happened
  after entry, above all contact with an advisor and integration into the
  department [lovitts2001].
- Departments and faculty explain departure with a deficit model, the student
  who left often adopts the same explanation, and because departures are not
  tracked the pattern stays invisible [lovitts2001].
- Students arrive with an inaccurate picture of what a doctorate involves; the
  gap is largest in the first year and again at the move from structured
  coursework to unstructured research, and both are attrition peaks
  [lovitts2001].
- Delamont and colleagues make the same point about tacit expectation: what a
  literature review is for, how much reading is enough, what a chapter draft
  looks like, and how often to report are rarely said out loud, and students
  cannot meet expectations they cannot see
  [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate].
- Wisker starts supervision from a negotiated learning contract covering roles,
  meeting frequency, draft exchange, turnaround times, what the supervisor
  will and will not do, and how disagreements are handled, revisited at each
  stage transition [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Masters and Kreeger observe that a development plan alone does not address
  the day-to-day operation of a lab, which is where many conflicts start, and
  that this is why they add a lab-specific expectations document
  [masters2017].

### Assessing understanding and handing over independence

- Entering Mentoring separates assessing understanding from checking status:
  mentees rarely say when they do not understand, so the mentor asks them to
  explain the plan or the result back in their own words [handelsman2005].
- Independence is framed as a deliberate hand-over: close direction and
  structured tasks at the start, stepping back as the mentee shows judgment,
  rather than a fixed personal style [handelsman2005].
- Wisker describes stuck places and conceptual thresholds: a student who
  appears stalled is often mid-crossing, and the supervisory move is to name
  the threshold and scaffold it rather than demand more output
  [wisker-good-supervisor].
- Wisker also finds that supervision drifts toward telling and status
  checking, and that the questions which move the work are the ones asking
  the student to justify a choice or connect a result to the argument
  [wisker-good-supervisor].

## Rules we adopt

1. Treat mentoring as a set of named competencies (expectations,
   communication, checking understanding, diversity, independence, ethics,
   philosophy) and check drafts and reviews against that list rather than
   against a general impression. (from [handelsman2005], [pfund2006])
2. Write expectations down before the relationship needs them, at onboarding,
   and again whenever the project, funding or role changes. An unwritten
   expectation is not an expectation. (from [masters2017], [lovitts2001],
   [delamont-supervising-the-doctorate])
3. Record the student's mentoring network in the IDP: at least one person
   besides Robert for method help and one for career paths he does not
   represent. A plan naming only the supervisor is incomplete.
   (from [nap2019-mentorship], [lee2007-nature-mentors])
4. When a student falls behind, examine structure first: meeting frequency,
   clarity of the plan, blocked resources, desk and group integration, and
   the coursework-to-research transition. Ability and motivation are never
   the first hypothesis and never a written one. (from [lovitts2001],
   [nap2019-mentorship])
5. Plan the hand-over to independence explicitly and record where the student
   is on it, so that the level of direction is a decision rather than a
   habit. (from [handelsman2005], [wisker-good-supervisor])
6. In every review, check understanding by asking the student to explain the
   plan or result back, and record what they said as evidence for the
   independence dimension. Never treat silence as agreement.
   (from [handelsman2005], [wisker-good-supervisor])
7. Keep the mentoring documents separate from assessment. The compact and the
   IDP support the relationship; scores and evaluations live in
   progress-review reports and are never delivered to the student through
   borg-cube. (from [nap2019-mentorship], [masters2017])
8. Review the compact and the IDP on a fixed date every twelve months and
   record the review even when nothing changed, so the cadence does not
   depend on whether anything went wrong. (from [masters2017],
   [pcbi-2022-mentorship-programme])
9. Track milestone outcomes, delays and departures for every person in the
   group and review them together once a year, because a pattern in one
   supervisor's students is invisible from single cases. (from [lovitts2001])
10. Name the mentoring relationship's power asymmetry in the compact itself,
    including how the student raises a problem with someone other than
    Robert. A compact without a route outside the dyad is not ready.
    (from [nap2019-mentorship], [masters2017])

## Where sources disagree

- Single mentor versus network: the older supervision literature centres the
  supervisor and treats other contacts as support
  [wisker-good-supervisor, delamont-supervising-the-doctorate], while the
  National Academies argue for a deliberate mentoring network
  [nap2019-mentorship]. We follow the network view and record it in the IDP,
  because a KAUST student's supervisor also controls funding and progression,
  which is exactly the concentration of roles the report warns about.
- Strength of the evidence: the National Academies present mentorship
  outcomes as an established association [nap2019-mentorship], while
  themselves reporting that the studies are mostly correlational and
  self-reported [nap2019-mentorship]. We use the report for what to do, not
  for effect sizes, and we do not quote numbers from it.
- What belongs in a written document: Masters and Kreeger want lab-specific
  operating rules in the expectations document [masters2017]; Wisker's
  learning contract is about roles and process [wisker-good-supervisor];
  the National Academies focus on the relationship [nap2019-mentorship]. We
  put operating rules and process in the compact and keep career direction in
  the IDP, so that one document can be shared with a new person unchanged.
- Programme-level versus dyad-level advice:
  [pcbi-2022-mentorship-programme] is written for people running a mentorship
  programme with many pairs, not for a single supervisor. We take its
  matching, communication and evaluation rules as advice for the group's
  internal mentoring (senior student to junior student) and ignore its
  funding and governance rules.

## Not covered

- Effect sizes, and whether any of the reported associations survive
  controlling for selection into good groups. None of the fetched sources
  gives a causal estimate for research supervision.
- Mentoring in a Saudi and KAUST context specifically: all the studies are
  from the US, UK and international online programmes.
- What to do when the supervisor is also the sole funder and the sole
  assessor, which is the normal case here.
- How to mentor across a large group where most day-to-day mentoring is done
  by postdocs and senior students who are themselves untrained.
- The Pfund 2006, Lee 2007 and HHMI/BWF entries were not fetched; their
  claims here come from the book notes and from general knowledge and need
  checking against the sources before a decision rests on them alone.
