# The Craft of Scientific Presentations

Source: Alley M (2013) The Craft of Scientific Presentations: Critical Steps to Succeed and Critical Errors to Avoid, 2nd ed. Springer
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- The book is organized around critical errors that scientists and engineers
  make in talks and the steps that avoid them: not knowing the audience,
  drowning the audience in detail, losing the audience in the structure,
  weak visual aids, reading slides, and poor delivery under pressure.
- Speech, structure, visual aids and delivery are treated as four separable
  components. Most failed talks are structural or visual failures rather than
  delivery failures, so preparation time should go to the message and the
  slides before rehearsal of voice and gesture.
- The assertion-evidence structure for slides: each slide carries one
  headline that is a complete sentence stating the slide's message (the
  assertion), set in no more than two lines and left-justified, supported by
  visual evidence (a figure, an image, an equation, a table, a diagram) rather
  than a bulleted list. Words in the body are kept to what the visual needs,
  such as labels or a short callout.
- Bulleted lists are the default of the software, not a communication
  choice. They hide the logical relationship between items, invite the
  speaker to read, and give the audience nothing to remember. The book
  argues that a sentence headline forces the speaker to know the point of
  the slide, and that audiences retain a sentence far better than a phrase.
- Typography guidance: sans-serif type, large sizes (about 28 point for the
  headline, 18 to 24 point for body text), high contrast, no all-capitals,
  minimal decoration in the template so that the evidence dominates.
- Structure of a talk: an opening that gives the audience a reason to listen
  and states the scope, a mapping slide that previews the main sections in
  the order they will come, a middle divided into a few clearly signposted
  sections, and a conclusion that restates the main assertions. The audience
  should always know where they are in the talk.
- Rehearsal: rehearse standing up, out loud, with the actual slides and a
  timer, several times; rehearse the transitions between slides, because
  transitions are where speakers lose the thread; rehearse the first minute
  until it is automatic, since nerves are highest there.
- Delivery: speak to the audience, not the screen; use the sentence
  headline as the anchor for what to say instead of notes; pause after each
  assertion; handle questions by restating the question, answering the part
  you can, and saying plainly what you do not know.
- Evidence: the book reports classroom and controlled studies in which
  audiences shown assertion-evidence slides scored higher on comprehension
  and retention tests than audiences shown topic-and-bullet slides for the
  same spoken content, and reports that speakers who build assertion-evidence
  slides understand their own material better.

## What we take from it

- Every slide title is a sentence that states the slide's message; the body
  is evidence for that sentence. This is the same rule as Robert's statement
  titles.
- Bullet lists are a last resort, not a default; nested bullets on one slide
  are an admission that the slide has no visual.
- A mapping slide near the start and signposts between sections keep the
  audience oriented; a talk that skips them loses people at transitions.
- Rehearse transitions and the first minute, not just the whole run;
  time every rehearsal.
- Large type and high contrast are non-negotiable; if the evidence cannot be
  read from the back of the room it is not evidence.
