# Leaving the Ivory Tower: The Causes and Consequences of Departure from Doctoral Study

Source: Lovitts BE (2001) Leaving the Ivory Tower: The Causes and Consequences of Departure from Doctoral Study. Rowman & Littlefield
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- A sociological study of doctoral attrition at two US research universities
  in the 1990s, combining surveys and interviews of completers and
  non-completers across several departments, plus interviews with faculty
  and directors of graduate study. Roughly half of entering US doctoral
  students did not finish, and the rate had been stable for decades.
- The central finding: completers and leavers were not distinguishable by
  ability. Entering credentials (test scores, prior grades) were similar
  across the two groups. What differed was the experience after entry.
- Lovitts adapts a model of student departure from undergraduate education:
  students who leave are less integrated, academically and socially, into
  their department. Leavers knew fewer faculty, had less contact with their
  advisor, were less likely to have a desk or office in the department, and
  were less likely to hold assistantships that put them in daily contact
  with others.
- The advisor relationship is the single strongest factor. Many leavers had
  no advisor, an advisor chosen by default, or an advisor who was distant or
  unresponsive. Some faculty lose far more students than others, but
  departments rarely know this because they do not keep records.
- Students arrive with an inaccurate mental map of what a doctorate involves;
  the gap between expectation and reality is largest in the first year and
  again at the transition from structured coursework to the unstructured
  dissertation stage. Both points are attrition peaks.
- Departments and faculty operate a deficit model: they assume that the
  student who left lacked ability or commitment. Students who leave often
  adopt the same explanation and blame themselves. Because departments do
  not track who leaves or why, the pattern stays invisible. Lovitts calls
  this shared misperception pluralistic ignorance.
- Funding structure matters through integration, not money alone: fellowship
  holders with no duties can be more isolated than teaching or research
  assistants who share offices and meetings.
- Leaving has real costs: leavers report shame, damaged confidence,
  financial loss and years without a credential; institutions lose the
  investment and rarely learn from it.
- Recommendations: give accurate information before and at entry; assign an
  advisor early and check the match; build cohort and community structures
  (shared space, regular seminars, writing groups); add structure to the
  dissertation stage (regular meetings, interim deadlines); collect and
  publish attrition data by department and by advisor; hold faculty
  accountable for supervision outcomes.

## What we take from it

- When a student falls behind, examine integration and structure first, not
  ability: how often do they meet their supervisor, do they have a desk in
  the group, do they attend group meetings, do they know what is expected.
- The transition from coursework to independent research is a predictable
  danger point; schedule extra supervision and a written plan there.
- Record milestone outcomes, delays and departures for every student and
  review them across the group each year, so patterns become visible.
- Make expectations explicit and written from day one; an inaccurate mental
  map of the doctorate is a cause of departure, not a personal failing.
- Peer structures (group meetings, code review, writing circles) are
  retention tools, not optional extras.
