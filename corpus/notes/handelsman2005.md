# Entering Mentoring: A Seminar to Train a New Generation of Scientists

Source: Handelsman J, Pfund C, Lauffer SM, Pribbenow CM (2005) Entering Mentoring: A Seminar to Train a New Generation of Scientists. University of Wisconsin Press
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- A facilitator's manual for a seminar that trains graduate students and
  postdocs who mentor undergraduate researchers, developed at the University
  of Wisconsin-Madison within the Wisconsin Program for Scientific Teaching.
  It is the origin of the Entering Mentoring curriculum later generalized by
  CIMER for faculty and other mentor groups.
- The premise: mentoring is a skill, learned like any other laboratory skill,
  and scientists are normally never taught it. Left untrained, mentors
  reproduce whatever they experienced, good or bad.
- The seminar runs as a series of roughly hour-long weekly sessions across
  a summer research program, so the mentors are mentoring while they train
  and bring live problems to the room. Learning is process-based: case
  studies, structured discussion and reflection, not lectures.
- Sessions are organized by theme: getting started and establishing
  expectations; communication and the mentee's understanding; addressing
  diversity and its effect on the relationship; fostering independence;
  ethics and professional conduct in research; and writing a personal
  mentoring philosophy as the closing assignment.
- Each theme has a set of case studies that pose a realistic dilemma (a
  mentee who is silent when confused, a mentee who works fewer hours than
  expected, credit for a result, a mentor who does the work for the mentee)
  and a set of discussion questions. The manual gives facilitators notes on
  what usually surfaces in discussion.
- Expectation setting is treated as a written act: mentors draft what they
  expect of the mentee and what the mentee can expect of them, and the manual
  supplies worksheets for that conversation. It also asks the mentor to
  define, with the mentee, what the project's success looks like.
- Assessing understanding is separated from checking status: the manual
  argues that mentees rarely say when they do not understand, so the mentor
  must ask them to explain the plan or the result back in their own words.
- Fostering independence is framed as a deliberate hand-over: the mentor
  starts with close direction and structured tasks and steps back as the
  mentee shows judgment, rather than choosing a fixed style.
- The mentoring philosophy assignment asks each participant to write down,
  in a page, how they mentor and why, so that practice becomes something
  they can articulate, revisit and be held to.
- The authors evaluated the seminar by comparing trained and untrained
  mentors of undergraduates (reported in Pfund et al. 2006, Science):
  trained mentors reported discussing expectations more often, thought more
  about diversity in the relationship and felt more confident.

## What we take from it

- Treat mentoring as a trainable competency with named components
  (expectations, communication, understanding, diversity, independence,
  ethics, philosophy) and check a supervisor's practice against each one.
- Put expectations in writing early and define what success on the project
  looks like, with the student present.
- In 1:1s, ask the student to explain the plan or result back; do not rely
  on "any questions?" as a check of understanding.
- Plan the hand-over to independence explicitly: more structure at the
  start, less as judgment shows; record where the student is on that path.
- Each supervisor writes a short mentoring philosophy; it feeds the
  expectations document and is revisited when the relationship shows strain.
