# Multimedia Learning

Source: Mayer RE (2020) Multimedia Learning, 3rd ed. Cambridge University Press
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- The cognitive theory of multimedia learning rests on three assumptions:
  people process words and pictures in two separate channels (auditory or
  verbal, and visual or pictorial); each channel has a limited capacity; and
  learning requires active processing (selecting, organizing, integrating).
- Three kinds of cognitive processing compete for that limited capacity:
  extraneous processing caused by poor design, essential processing needed
  to represent the material, and generative processing that makes sense of
  it. Good design cuts extraneous load, manages essential load and fosters
  generative processing.
- Principles for reducing extraneous processing: coherence (remove
  interesting but irrelevant words, pictures and sounds); signaling (add
  cues that highlight the organization, such as headings and arrows);
  redundancy (narration plus graphics beats narration plus graphics plus
  the same text on screen); spatial contiguity (put words next to the part
  of the picture they describe); temporal contiguity (present matching
  words and pictures at the same time).
- Principles for managing essential processing: segmenting (learner-paced
  pieces rather than one continuous unit); pre-training (introduce names and
  characteristics of the key components before the explanation); modality
  (spoken words with graphics beat printed words with graphics when pacing
  is fast).
- Principles for fostering generative processing: multimedia (words plus
  pictures beat words alone); personalization (conversational rather than
  formal style); voice (a human voice beats a machine voice); embodiment
  (an on-screen instructor who gestures helps); the image principle notes
  that merely adding a static picture of the speaker does not help.
- Effects are measured with transfer tests and are consistently strongest
  for novices and for fast-paced, system-controlled presentations; experts
  are less affected and can be hurt by scaffolding they do not need.
- The evidence base is dozens of controlled experiments with median effect
  sizes reported per principle; the book also reviews boundary conditions
  where a principle does not hold (for example the redundancy principle
  reverses when the words are technical, the pace is slow, or the audience
  is non-native).

## What we take from it

- Slides are graphics with spoken words: do not put on the slide the
  sentences you will say (redundancy); keep the words that label the visual
  (spatial contiguity) and the one sentence that states the message
  (signaling).
- Remove decoration, side stories and unused panels from slides
  (coherence); one idea per slide is the segmenting principle applied.
- Define symbols, acronyms and baselines before the slide that uses them
  (pre-training); this is the evidence behind Robert's define-or-drop and
  no-forward-reference rules.
- Show the label next to the thing it labels, not in a legend across the
  slide, and show a figure at the moment it is spoken about, not before.
- Expect the rules to matter most for audiences new to the topic (students,
  general conference audiences); a specialist committee tolerates denser
  slides, but the redundancy principle still holds.
