# The Visual Display of Quantitative Information

Source: Tufte ER (2001) The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, 2nd ed. Graphics Press
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- Graphical excellence is the well-designed presentation of interesting
  data: it gives the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest
  time with the least ink in the smallest space, and it tells the truth
  about the data.
- Data-ink ratio: the share of a graphic's ink that represents data. Erase
  non-data ink and redundant data ink; every mark should carry information.
  Heavy frames, gridlines, backgrounds and three-dimensional effects are
  the usual offenders.
- Chartjunk: decoration that carries no data (moire patterns, heavy grids,
  self-promoting "ducks" where the design of the graphic dominates its
  content). Chartjunk is never an improvement; if a graphic is dull, the
  data or the design of the comparison is at fault.
- Graphical integrity: the size of an effect shown in the graphic should
  match the size of the effect in the data (the "lie factor"); show data in
  context; label directly; do not use more dimensions in the drawing than in
  the data; avoid truncated axes that exaggerate change without saying so.
- Small multiples: a series of small graphics with the same design and
  scales, showing changes across one variable, lets the viewer compare
  many cases at once; this is one of the most powerful designs for
  scientific data.
- High data density is a virtue: printed graphics can show far more than
  most authors attempt, and viewers can read dense, well-organized displays
  (maps, timetables) without difficulty.
- Friendly graphics: words spelled out rather than abbreviated, labels on
  the graphic rather than in a legend, type set horizontally, colors chosen
  so that color-blind viewers can read them, and a design that respects the
  viewer's intelligence.
- Historical examples (Playfair, Minard, Marey) show that clarity, layered
  detail and a strong comparison, not novelty of form, make a graphic
  memorable.

## What we take from it

- Remove non-data ink from every figure destined for a paper or a slide:
  frames, gridlines, backgrounds, three-dimensional effects, redundant tick
  labels.
- Label directly on the plot instead of using a separate legend where
  space allows; set text horizontally.
- Never let the visual effect exceed the data effect: full axes, or a
  visible break and a caption sentence when an axis is truncated.
- Use small multiples with shared axes for comparisons across conditions,
  seeds or datasets, rather than one cluttered overlay or several
  inconsistent panels.
- A figure that needs explaining in a slide caption for something the pixels
  do not show is a figure to redesign, not to caption around.
