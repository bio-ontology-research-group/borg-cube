# Understanding by Design

Source: Wiggins G, McTighe J (2005) Understanding by Design, expanded 2nd ed. ASCD
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- Backward design: plan a course or unit in three stages. Stage one fixes
  the desired results (what students should understand and be able to do).
  Stage two decides what evidence would show that those results were
  reached (the assessment). Stage three, and only then, plans the learning
  activities. Most teachers plan in the opposite order, starting from a
  textbook or from favorite activities, which the authors call the twin sins
  of activity-oriented and coverage-oriented design.
- Desired results are layered: enduring understandings (big ideas worth
  keeping years later), essential questions that keep the inquiry open,
  and the knowledge and skills needed to get there. Content standards are
  unpacked into these three layers rather than copied into the syllabus.
- Understanding is not a single thing. The book proposes six facets:
  explanation, interpretation, application, perspective, empathy and
  self-knowledge. A student who can only recall has not shown
  understanding; assessments should ask for more than one facet.
- Assessment evidence should be collected as a range, from informal checks
  and quizzes to performance tasks. Authentic performance tasks are framed
  with the GRASPS elements (goal, role, audience, situation, product or
  performance, standards) so the task resembles how the knowledge is used
  outside school. Rubrics make the standards explicit before the task is set.
- The learning plan is checked against the WHERETO questions: where the
  unit is going and why, hooking and holding interest, equipping students
  through experiences and instruction, giving chances to rethink and revise,
  letting students evaluate their own work, tailoring to different needs,
  and organizing the sequence for deep engagement.
- Curricular priorities: distinguish what is worth being familiar with, what
  is important to know and do, and what merits enduring understanding, and
  spend time in proportion.
- A design standards checklist lets peers review a unit before it is taught,
  which is itself a form of backward design applied to the teacher's work.

## What we take from it

- Course design starts from a written list of outcomes and the evidence that
  would satisfy them; activities and slides come last. The course-design
  skill therefore builds course.yaml (outcomes, assessments, weeks) before
  any lecture material.
- Every outcome needs at least one assessment that produces evidence of
  more than recall; the alignment matrix makes the gap visible.
- Essential questions and enduring understandings belong in the syllabus so
  students see why the material matters.
- Performance tasks framed for a real audience and situation suit our
  project-based courses; rubrics are published with the task.
- Review the design against the WHERETO questions once the week plan exists,
  and revise before the semester, not during it.
