# A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives

Source: Anderson LW, Krathwohl DR (eds) (2001) A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing: A Revision of Bloom's Taxonomy of Educational Objectives. Longman
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- The revision replaces the single 1956 list of six noun categories with a
  two-dimensional table. An educational objective is a verb plus a noun: the
  verb names a cognitive process, the noun names the kind of knowledge.
- The knowledge dimension has four types. Factual: terminology and specific
  details (gene names, file formats, a benchmark's numbers). Conceptual:
  classifications, principles, theories and models (why a baseline is needed,
  what a confounder is). Procedural: how to do something, methods, and the
  criteria for choosing a method (how to run the pipeline, when to use which
  test). Metacognitive: knowledge about one's own thinking, about strategies,
  and about the task, including knowing what one does not know.
- The cognitive process dimension has six categories, ordered from less to
  more complex, and each has named sub-processes. Remember: recognizing,
  recalling. Understand: interpreting, exemplifying, classifying,
  summarizing, inferring, comparing, explaining. Apply: executing a familiar
  procedure, implementing a procedure in an unfamiliar task. Analyze:
  differentiating relevant from irrelevant parts, organizing parts into a
  structure, attributing a point of view or purpose. Evaluate: checking for
  internal consistency, critiquing against external criteria. Create:
  generating hypotheses, planning a procedure, producing a product.
- The hierarchy is loose: categories overlap and a person can evaluate
  without having first applied. Retention needs remembering; transfer to new
  problems needs the other five.
- Alignment is the working tool: objectives, learning activities and
  assessments should sit in the same cell of the table. A mismatch (teach
  procedures, assess recall) is the commonest design fault.
- The book's second half is a set of vignettes in which real lesson plans are
  placed in the table to show how classification exposes gaps, for example a
  unit with many remember-factual cells and no evaluate or create cell.
- The authors intend the table for planning and for diagnosing what students
  can actually do, not for grading people. A cell describes a task, not a
  person.

## What we take from it

- Judge the level of a student's reasoning by the verb they can honestly
  perform on their own work, not by the vocabulary they use. A student who
  can recall the method (remember) is at a different level from one who can
  explain why it is the right method here (understand, conceptual), predict
  what would break it (analyze), or judge whether the result supports the
  claim (evaluate).
- Write pre-1:1 questions as verb plus noun pairs and vary the cell. Examples:
  "Explain why the baseline was chosen" (understand, conceptual); "What would
  you change if the data were half the size?" (apply or analyze, procedural);
  "Which of your three results would you defend least, and why?" (evaluate,
  metacognitive); "Design the experiment that would falsify your claim"
  (create, conceptual).
- Metacognitive questions are the independence signal: a student who can say
  what they do not yet know, and what they will do about it, is planning
  their own work.
- Avoid stacking questions in one cell. A meeting that only asks "what did you
  do" tests remember and apply and never reaches evaluate or create, so it
  cannot tell whether the student understands their own project.
- Record the level reached as evidence for the independence dimension, with
  the date and the question, never as a label on the student.
