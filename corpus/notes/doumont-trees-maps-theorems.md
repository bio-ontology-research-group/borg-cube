# Trees, Maps, and Theorems: Effective Communication for Rational Minds

Source: Doumont JL (2009) Trees, Maps, and Theorems: Effective Communication for Rational Minds. Principiae
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- Three laws of communication apply to every document, talk and figure:
  adapt to the audience; maximize the signal-to-noise ratio; use effective
  redundancy. Everything else in the book is an application of these laws.
- Communication is judged by its effect on the audience, not by what the
  author put in. The message must be stated, not left for the audience to
  reconstruct; the first thing a reader or listener needs is the conclusion
  and why it matters to them.
- Trees: hierarchical structure. A talk or paper should have a small number
  of top-level points (typically three to five), each with its own
  subordinate points, and the structure must be visible to the audience
  through explicit previews, transitions and reviews.
- Maps: the audience needs to know where they are. Documents use headings,
  tables of contents and summaries; talks use an explicit outline (the
  "map") announced at the start, restated at transitions, and closed at the
  end. Messages come before details ("message first, then support").
- Theorems: a message is a statement that can be true or false, not a topic.
  Headings, slide titles and figure captions should be full-sentence
  messages, and the supporting material should prove the theorem the title
  states.
- Slides: one message per slide, stated as a sentence at the top; the body
  proves it visually; text on slides is kept minimal because reading and
  listening compete. Slides are for the audience, not notes for the speaker;
  a separate handout serves those who want the details.
- Oral presentations: the opening must answer why the audience should
  listen (context, need, task, object of the talk), the body follows the
  announced map, the closing restates the main messages and says what
  should happen next. Questions are part of the talk and deserve a
  rehearsed strategy.
- Figures and tables: the message determines the graph type; every visual
  element must carry meaning; labels replace legends when possible; the
  caption states the point of the figure, not just its content.
- Noise is anything that does not carry the message: decoration, filler
  words, redundant text, unexplained abbreviations. Effective redundancy is
  saying the same message in two channels (words and picture) so it survives
  a lapse in attention; ineffective redundancy is reading the slide aloud.

## What we take from it

- Slide titles and section headings are messages (statements), never topics;
  this is the theorem rule and matches Robert's statement-title rule.
- Open with context, need and the object of the talk so the audience knows
  why to listen; close by restating the messages.
- Make the structure visible: a map at the start, signposts between
  sections. A listener should be able to say at any moment which part of the
  talk they are in.
- Treat everything that does not carry the message as noise to be cut:
  decorative boxes, meta labels, filler openers.
- Redundancy is good only across channels (picture plus spoken sentence),
  bad within one channel (text read aloud).
