# Creating Significant Learning Experiences

Source: Fink LD (2013) Creating Significant Learning Experiences: An Integrated Approach to Designing College Courses, revised and updated ed. Jossey-Bass
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct. The freely downloadable 2003 self-directed guide (manifest id fink2003-self-directed-guide) is the condensed version of the same model and was fetched and read.

## Main ideas

- Learning is significant when it changes the learner in a lasting way.
  The taxonomy of significant learning names six kinds: foundational
  knowledge (understanding and remembering), application (skills; critical,
  creative and practical thinking; managing projects), integration
  (connecting ideas, people and realms of life), the human dimension
  (learning about oneself and others), caring (new feelings, interests,
  values) and learning how to learn (becoming a better student, inquiring,
  self-direction). The kinds interact: pursuing one strengthens the others.
- The taxonomy is deliberately non-hierarchical, unlike the cognitive
  taxonomies, so that a course can set goals in several kinds at once
  instead of climbing a ladder.
- Integrated course design rests on four components that must fit each
  other: situational factors, learning goals, feedback and assessment, and
  teaching and learning activities. A course is well designed when the
  goals reflect the situation, the assessment measures every goal, the
  activities lead students to every goal, and the practice-and-feedback
  loop prepares students for the assessment.
- Situational factors to review before setting goals: class size and level,
  meeting pattern and delivery mode, expectations from the institution and
  profession, nature of the subject (theoretical or practical, convergent or
  divergent), learner characteristics and prior knowledge, and the teacher's
  own beliefs and strengths.
- Educative assessment goes beyond auditing for a grade: forward-looking
  tasks set in the situations where the knowledge will be used, explicit
  criteria and standards, student self-assessment, and feedback that is
  frequent, immediate, discriminating and delivered with care.
- Active learning has three components that a full set of activities must
  cover: getting information and ideas, experience (doing and observing,
  real or simulated) and reflective dialogue (with oneself and with others).
  Direct forms are preferred to vicarious ones where possible.
- Course structure means 4 to 7 major topics in sequence with assignments
  that grow in complexity; an instructional strategy sequences in-class and
  out-of-class activities so that energy accumulates (the castle-top
  pattern); together they give the week-by-week schedule.
- Later chapters cover grading that reflects the goals without grading
  everything, debugging the design for workload and resource problems, a
  syllabus that communicates the design, evaluating the course while it
  runs, and the institutional conditions that let faculty redesign courses.

## What we take from it

- Start every course design with the situational factors and with goals in
  more than one kind of significant learning; a course whose outcomes are all
  "understand and remember" is incomplete.
- Every learning goal in course.yaml has both an assessment and an activity
  that leads to it; the alignment matrix checks this mechanically.
- Assessments are forward-looking where the subject allows: tasks set in the
  situation a researcher or engineer would meet, with published criteria.
- Week plans alternate in-class and out-of-class work so that practice with
  feedback precedes every graded task.
- Grade weights mirror the importance of the goals; not every activity is
  graded.
- Plan the course evaluation (mid-semester feedback and end-of-semester
  review) as part of the design, not as an afterthought.
