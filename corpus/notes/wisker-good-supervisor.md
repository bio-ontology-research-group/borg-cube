# The Good Supervisor: Supervising Postgraduate and Undergraduate Research for Doctoral Theses and Dissertations

Source: Wisker G (2012) The Good Supervisor: Supervising Postgraduate and Undergraduate Research for Doctoral Theses and Dissertations, 2nd ed. Palgrave Macmillan
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- A comprehensive handbook for research supervisors across levels and
  disciplines, grounded in Wisker's own research on supervisory dialogues
  and on how research students make conceptual progress.
- Supervision begins with a negotiated learning contract or set of ground
  rules: roles, meeting frequency, how drafts are exchanged, turnaround
  times, what the supervisor will and will not do, how disagreements are
  handled. The contract is revisited at stage transitions.
- Supervisory dialogue is the core skill. Wisker distinguishes didactic
  talk (telling), domestic talk (checking in on routine and welfare) and
  dialogic talk that prompts the student to think, and argues that most
  supervisions drift toward telling and checking. Open questions that ask
  the student to justify a choice or connect a result to the argument are
  what move the work.
- Students hit stuck places and conceptual thresholds; crossing them
  produces a learning leap in how they understand the research (for
  example moving from describing methods to arguing why the design answers
  the question). Supervisors should recognize the threshold, name it and
  scaffold the crossing rather than push for output.
- Supervision styles vary from directive to hands-off and students vary in
  what they need; a mismatch that goes unnamed produces frustration on both
  sides. Wisker recommends discussing style openly and adjusting it over
  time as the student gains independence.
- Stages of the research have different supervisory tasks: shaping the
  question and proposal, methodology and design, data and analysis,
  writing, and preparing for examination. The book gives prompts and
  checklists for each.
- Writing is supported through structured drafting, feedback that addresses
  argument before surface, and peer writing groups; Wisker also uses group
  supervision and student cohorts to reduce isolation.
- The book treats wellbeing and workload directly: students' lives
  interrupt research, supervisors should notice changes in engagement and
  refer to institutional support, and part-time, international and distance
  students need adjusted contact patterns.
- Examining and the viva are covered as part of supervision: mock vivas,
  understanding examiner expectations, and writing for the reader who
  judges the thesis.
- Supervisor development: reflective practice, peer supervision groups and
  formal training are recommended; supervising well is presented as a
  professional skill that improves with feedback.

## What we take from it

- Agree a learning contract at the start and revisit it at each stage
  transition; this is the expectations document.
- Plan 1:1s so that dialogic questions (why this design, how does this
  result bear on the claim) take more time than status checking.
- Recognize conceptual thresholds as the reason a student appears stuck,
  and scaffold the crossing instead of demanding output.
- Name the supervision style in use and adjust it deliberately over time.
- Treat noticing changes in engagement and referring to support as part of
  the supervisor's job, while leaving diagnosis to professionals.
