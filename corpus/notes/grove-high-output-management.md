# High Output Management

Source: Grove AS (1995) High Output Management, 2nd ed. Vintage
Status: drafted from general knowledge of the work on 2026-09-02; cite-only book; Robert to review and correct.

## Main ideas

- A manager's output is the output of the organization under them plus the
  output of neighboring organizations they influence. Individual effort counts
  only through its effect on that output.
- Grove applies production thinking to knowledge work: find the limiting step,
  schedule around it, batch where it helps, and catch defects at the point
  where the least value has been added (the cheapest place to fix them).
- Indicators make work visible. Grove wants a small set of measurements per
  process, each paired with a counter-indicator (quantity with quality,
  throughput with error rate) so that optimizing one does not silently damage
  the other. Leading indicators and trend charts matter more than snapshots.
- Managerial leverage: every managerial activity has a leverage, the amount of
  output it changes per unit of the manager's time. High-leverage activities
  affect many people or have a long-lasting effect (training, setting
  direction early, a well-timed decision). Negative leverage exists too: a
  delayed decision, meddling in details a subordinate owns, or a manager whose
  mood depresses a whole team.
- Delegation is a high-leverage activity only with monitoring. Delegating
  without a way to check output is abdication. Monitor at the lowest
  value-added stage and sample rather than inspect everything.
- Task-relevant maturity (TRM): how much structure a person needs depends on
  their experience with the specific task, not on seniority or general
  ability. Low TRM calls for precise, structured instruction (what, when, how);
  medium TRM for two-way communication and support; high TRM for agreed
  objectives and light monitoring. TRM is task-specific and can drop when the
  task or the environment changes, so the style must shift with it.
- Meetings are the medium of managerial work. Process-oriented meetings
  (one-on-ones, staff meetings, operation reviews) run on a regular schedule;
  mission-oriented meetings exist to make one decision and should be rare.
- The one-on-one is the subordinate's meeting. The subordinate prepares the
  agenda and brings the problems; the supervisor listens, asks one more
  question, and coaches. Frequency follows TRM (weekly for new tasks, less
  often for mastered ones); duration should be long enough for hard topics to
  surface; both sides keep notes and a running list of items to bring next
  time.
- Decision making: free discussion, then a clear decision, then full support.
  Before deciding, fix what decision is needed, by when, who decides, who is
  consulted, who ratifies or can veto, and who must be informed.
- Planning means comparing what the environment will demand with what the
  organization is on track to deliver, then choosing today's actions to close
  tomorrow's gap. Objectives and key results (MBO) express this: few
  objectives, short horizons, milestones that show whether you are on pace,
  and nested objectives so each level supports the one above.
- Training is the manager's job and cannot be delegated away; a small amount of
  well-prepared teaching multiplies across every task the trainee does later.
- Performance review is task-relevant: judge output and internal measures over
  the period, keep the message to what the person can act on, and separate
  assessment of performance from guesses about potential.

## What we take from it

- Measure delegated work by indicators of output (a working script, a figure,
  a section drafted), never by hours or activity reports.
- Write two indicators for anything we track (for example count of items done
  and count of items reopened) so that speed is not bought with rework.
- Scale the detail of a brief to the delegate's task-relevant maturity: a first
  task in a new area gets what, when, how and a worked example; a task the
  person has done before gets an objective and a check date.
- Every delegated item has a monitoring point at the cheapest stage: a plan or
  a first result reviewed early, not a finished product reviewed late.
- One-on-ones are scheduled, not ad hoc; the student owns the agenda; the
  supervisor's notes feed the next meeting's action list.
- Decisions taken in a meeting record who decided and who must be told;
  otherwise they are not decisions.
- Planning starts from the deadline and the gap, not from the list of things
  we could do; the plan names a few objectives and the milestones that show
  whether we are on pace.
